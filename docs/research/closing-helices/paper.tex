\begin{abstract}
Automatic quad remeshing often produces edge loops that wind round a part of
the shape as helices instead of closing into rings. Bommes et
al.~\cite{bommes2011structure} remove such \emph{q-helices} after meshing;
we prevent them where the integer structure of the mesh is decided, in the
quantization of a T-mesh. A straight walk across the rectangles of a T-mesh
that returns to its first side, a \emph{turn}, has a \emph{shift} that is
an integer linear form in the arc lengths; at integer lengths it is how far
the dual line following the same patches comes back beside itself. We add
the constraints that close turns to a minimum-deviation
quantizer~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf}, through a least-change projection,
repair walks and rounds of constraint generation on the strips of the mesh, and keep the
candidate with the fewest spiral edges. On twelve models at three budgets
near 3{,}000 quads, the engine leaves 4.65\% of the edges on spiral loops
(16.7\% without closing, in single runs of an earlier engine); on a human
body, single runs give 1.6\%, against 2.5\% for the hand-made mesh. Reading
the shift as translational holonomy of the flat metric, we show that it is
the Burgers vector of the singularities on one side of the turn, that a
dislocation joined by a staircase of arcs between two turns forbids closing
both, and that on a closed sphere the shift of a turn has the parity of its
length. Turned around, the conflicts bound from below the number of turns
of a family that every quantization within given bounds leaves shifted: on
the body, within the engine's bounds of separation, one to nine of the turns
it holds, where it leaves 13 to 123 shifted; with its deviation cap and
window of sizes as well, exactly one in two ways where it leaves 13 and 16,
and in the others certified ranges from 1--12 to 9--90. Choosing the turns to close is
NP-hard. The algebraic cores of the proofs are checked in Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Field-aligned quad remeshing has reached a level where element shape and
alignment are rarely the limiting factor~\cite{bommes2013survey,campen2017survey}. What remains is structure: the
way edge loops run across the surface. Artists expect a loop round an arm,
a leg or a neck to be a ring. Automatic methods often produce a helix
instead: the loop goes round, comes back one or a few rows further, and
winds along the whole part. The problem is old and well identified:
Bommes et al.\ showed in 2011 that helical configurations are a main reason why
the base complex of an otherwise good quad mesh stays dense~\cite{bommes2011structure}.

This note documents an approach developed in the open-source remesher
openclare, and positions it against the literature we could check. What we
claim as new, as far as we found, is listed first; the known results it
builds on are attributed where they are used.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Turns} of a T-mesh and their \emph{shift}, a linear form in the
  arc lengths of the T-mesh that gives, at integer lengths and within the
  corridor of the turn, the pitch of the dual line that follows it
  (Section~\ref{sec:turns}). That a translational period of a flat metric is
  linear in the lengths is classical; its form on a T-mesh, as a constraint
  of the quantization, is what is new.
\item Closing turns within T-mesh quantization: a least-change projection
  of the target lengths, an integer search, repair walks judged on the
  turns, rounds of constraint generation on the strips of the mesh, and a choice among
  candidates by the share of spiral edges, with safeguards on folds,
  distortion and budget (Section~\ref{sec:closing}); measured on a bench
  that holds the result against open-source remeshers and a mesh modelled by
  hand (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item A share of spiral edges that tells helices of pitch up to four from
  closed, open and border loops (Section~\ref{sec:measure}); loop counts and
  loop simplicity scores exist~\cite{liu2025quadgpt,kong2026squadgen}, but
  they do not separate helices from open loops.
\item Obstructions to closing, read on turns: the shift of a turn is the
  Burgers vector of the singularities on one side, in the order of first
  visit of a cutting tree; a pair of valences three and five, or two
  vertices of equal valence exchanged, joined by a staircase of arcs between
  two turns, forces one of them to be shifted; a separatrix that winds once
  round and stops beside its own vertex does the same to a single turn; a
  checkerboard rule gives the parity of the shift between two turns, and on a
  closed sphere the shift of every turn has the parity of its length, at all
  balanced lengths; \emph{combs} write every certificate of a conflict on
  the body as local dislocations (Section~\ref{sec:obstructions}). Holonomy,
  Burgers vectors, dislocations as pairs of disclinations, the parity of
  cycles of quadrangulations and the Fox calculus are classical; their
  statements in terms of turns, staircases and combs are ours.
\item A lower bound on the number of turns of a family that every
  quantization within given bounds leaves shifted, by disjoint infeasible
  sets, with chains of dislocations as a corollary, computed exactly or
  within certified intervals on a human body and compared with the engine
  (Section~\ref{sec:forced}). The bound is the packing bound of a minimum
  cover of irreducible infeasible subsystems, a known notion; its use for
  helices is new. Choosing the turns to close is NP-hard: known for maximum
  feasible subsystems in general, shown here for the restricted instances of
  quantization (bidirected balances, bounds $0$ and $1$).
\item An exact look at whole lengths on the same body: the turns, not the
  rounding, decide what closes (Appendix~\ref{sec:whole}).
\item Machine-checked proofs, in Lean~4~\cite{demoura2021lean4} with
  Mathlib~\cite{mathlib2020}, of the algebraic and combinatorial cores of
  the proofs, with the topological facts as explicit hypotheses
  (Section~\ref{sec:lean}).
\end{enumerate}
The results are preliminary: single runs except where three budgets are
stated, one implementation, and
heuristics where an exact formulation is still missing
(Section~\ref{sec:open}). Statements are \emph{proved} when they come with a
proof, \emph{measured} when they come from exact arithmetic or solver output
on the systems openclare exports, and \emph{conjectured} otherwise; we say
which.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Counting singularities.}
On a closed surface of genus $g$, the valences $k(v)$ of a quad mesh
satisfy $\sum_v (4 - k(v)) = 4\chi = 8(1-g)$. With only valences 3 and 5,
$n_3 - n_5 = 8(1-g)$: a sphere or a closed human body needs at least eight
vertices of valence three. This bound says how many singular vertices are
needed, not where they go; a hand-made human base mesh has hundreds.

\paragraph{Quad meshes as quartic differentials.}
Lei et al.~\cite{lei2020quartic} show that a closed quad mesh induces a
Riemann surface and a meromorphic quartic differential $\omega = (dz)^4$
whose zeros and poles are the singular vertices, a vertex of valence $k$
being of order $k-4$; conversely a quartic differential with finite
trajectories induces a quad mesh. The divisor of singularities then
satisfies the Abel--Jacobi condition, and a divisor satisfying it is the
divisor of a meromorphic quartic differential. Zheng et
al.~\cite{zheng2020abeljacobi} optimize the singularity configuration by
integer programming to satisfy the condition. Two points matter here.
First, the condition is stated with holomorphic one-forms, which a surface
of genus zero does not have; our reading is that in genus zero it adds
nothing to the degree condition (this is an inference, not a statement of
the papers). Second, the paper lists as an open problem the guarantee that
all trajectories are \emph{finite}. A trajectory that is not finite is
precisely an edge loop that does not close: the helices of this note.

\paragraph{Closed trajectories.}
A quadratic differential all of whose non-critical horizontal trajectories
are closed is a Jenkins--Strebel differential~\cite{strebel1984quadratic};
the complement of its critical graph is a union of flat cylinders (ring
domains), each with a circumference and a height; we call \emph{twist} the
shift with which a cylinder is glued back after a cut along its core curve. In a quad mesh, a ring of quads is
such a cylinder; a helix is a cylinder whose gluing is shifted by a whole
number of rows. Section~\ref{sec:turns} makes this precise at the level of a
T-mesh.

\paragraph{Quad meshes as flat metrics.}
Declaring every quad a unit square turns a quad mesh into a flat metric
with cone points at its irregular vertices. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2019metric}
characterize the metrics that arise this way by four conditions:
Gauss--Bonnet, rotational holonomy in multiples of a quarter-turn,
alignment with the border, and a \emph{finite geodesic lamination}: the
straight lines of the field are finite loops. On a surface of genus zero
the holonomy condition follows from Gauss--Bonnet (Lemma~3.9 of the arXiv version of~\cite{chen2019metric}). The
translational part of the holonomy is not discussed, and the finiteness
condition is assumed, not constructed. Lei et
al.~\cite{lei2023crossfields} show that the singularity configurations of
quad meshes are a strict subset of those of cross fields, and explain in
this way why no quad mesh of the torus has exactly one vertex of valence
three and one of valence five.

\paragraph{Dislocations.}
Izmestiev et al.~\cite{izmestiev2013torus} give geometric proofs of such
non-existence results, among them the torus with one vertex of valence three
and one of valence five, proved combinatorially by Barnette, Jucovi\v{c}
and Trenkler~\cite{barnette1971toroidal}. Their tool is the holonomy of the
developing map of the flat metric, a rigid motion per loop. When the
rotation of a loop is the identity, its translation is the \emph{Burgers
vector} of crystallography; two opposite cone points at distinct positions
always have a non-zero one, which is the dislocation a pair of valences three
and five makes in the square grid. Section~\ref{sec:obstructions} uses this
tool for the shifts of turns.

\paragraph{Helices in quad meshes.}
Bommes et al.~\cite{bommes2011structure} define a \emph{q-helix} as a dual
parametric line of a quad mesh with an integer \emph{pitch} $h$ and
\emph{turn length} $\tau$: walking $\tau$ steps along it is equivalent to
$h$ side-steps. They detect minimal q-helices ($h \le \tau$, no sub-helix of
smaller pitch) by the self-intersections of orthogonal dual lines, and
remove them greedily with \emph{grid-preserving operators}: closed dual
paths of shift and collapse steps, found by a graph search, that keep the
mesh all-quad and its singularities in place. Tangential smoothing then
repairs the distortion the shifts introduce. Their experiments remove most
helices (from 17--52 down to 1--23 per model) and coarsen the base complex
accordingly. They note that choosing the pairs of singularities to align
directly in the parametrization, as Myles et al.\ do for
T-meshes~\cite{myles2010feature}, is often complicated or even ambiguous to
automate. The approach works on a finished mesh; it does not
touch the parametrization or its quantization. The closest prior work after
it is that of Razafindrazaka and Polthier~\cite{razafindrazaka2017optimal},
who start from a finished quad mesh and replace its base complex by an
optimal one, found as a perfect matching on a graph over its singular
vertices; they note that the operators of~\cite{bommes2011structure} leave
some spirals. Like those operators, this works after meshing, not in the
quantization.

\paragraph{T-mesh quantization.}
Mixed-integer quadrangulation~\cite{bommes2009miq} and integer-grid
maps~\cite{bommes2013igm} decide the integer structure of a quad mesh in
their quantization. Campen et al.\ quantize via the
integer lengths of the arcs of a T-mesh traced in a seamless
parametrization~\cite{campen2015qgp}. Lyon et al.\ solve a constrained
version as an integer linear program, bounding the directional deviation of
the separatrices of the resulting layout~\cite{lyon2021constrained}, and
require, as openclare does, that the quantization keep singularities apart.
Heistermann et al.\ cast quantization as a
minimum-deviation flow on a bi-directed graph and give a fast approximate
solver and an exact refinement~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf}; openclare
follows their formulation. To our knowledge none of these constrains the
helices: the lengths are chosen for consistency, validity and closeness to
the parametrization, and the loops close or spiral as a side effect.

\paragraph{Periods round a tube.}
QuadCover~\cite{kalberer2007quadcover} lifts the cross field to a branched
covering where it becomes a vector field, and integrates it there. The
stripe parameterization of tubular surfaces~\cite{kalberer2011stripe}, built
on it, measures the jump (``gap'') of the parameter across each cut path,
which is constant along the path, and rounds it to the nearest whole number;
the grid lines are continuous exactly when every gap is whole. The gap of
the coordinate across a loop round a tube is what we call the shift of a
turn: rounded to zero it gives rings, rounded to a non-zero whole number a
helix of that pitch. That paper treats stripes spiralling round the tube as
a legitimate outcome, and notes that rounding the gaps for least distortion
is a hard combinatorial problem that it does not solve exactly.

\paragraph{Layouts traced from the field.}
Myles et al.~\cite{myles2014robust} trace the field into a partition into
quadrilateral patches and adjust the layout until its lengths are
consistent; Pietroni et al.~\cite{pietroni2016tracing} select traced
separatrices by a binary linear program; Razafindrazaka et
al.~\cite{razafindrazaka2015perfect} obtain a layout as a minimum-weight
perfect matching on a graph of the singularities. Campen surveys these
methods~\cite{campen2017survey}. Except for the parametrization
of~\cite{myles2014robust}, they build coarse layouts; whether a dense mesh
made from the layout spirals is, as far as we read, not among their
criteria.

\paragraph{Lower bounds.}
We found no result that bounds from below the number of helices of a quad
mesh, or the number of its loops that do not close, for a given singularity
configuration. The nearest results are existence and non-existence theorems:
Strebel's existence of Jenkins--Strebel differentials with prescribed
cylinders on a fixed Riemann surface~\cite{strebel1984quadratic}, the
non-existence results above~\cite{izmestiev2013torus,lei2023crossfields},
and the finiteness condition of~\cite{chen2019metric}, which the open
problem of~\cite{lei2020quartic} asks to guarantee. Our bound is a minimum
cover of irreducible infeasible subsystems, a classical notion of linear
feasibility~\cite{parker1996iis,amaldi2003maxfs}, applied to the shifts of
turns (Section~\ref{sec:forced}).

\paragraph{Loops by construction.}
Dual loops meshing~\cite{campen2012dual} builds a layout from loops traced
on the surface, which close by construction; it produces coarse layouts
rather than dense field-aligned meshes. Lei et al.~\cite{lei2017foliation}
build quad meshes from a Strebel differential with prescribed cylinders, so
that every trajectory closes, and skeleton-guided
layouts~\cite{usai2015skeleton} close the loops round limbs by design. Both
produce coarse layouts, and neither starts from a given cross field and
singularity configuration.

\paragraph{Measuring loops.}
QuadGPT~\cite{liu2025quadgpt} counts \emph{quad rings} (closed loops of
faces) and \emph{quad lines} (open strips) and uses the share of faces in
rings and the mean length of lines as reward signals for preference
optimization; it does not report them as evaluation metrics, nor does it
tell spirals from open lines. SQuadGen~\cite{kong2026squadgen} scores the
face loops and edge loops of a layout by their self-intersections and their
rotation index (the total curvature of the loop, projected on a best-fit
plane, over $2\pi$), and reports the area share of simple loops; it does not
tell helices from open loops either.

\section{Turns of a T-mesh}\label{sec:turns}

\paragraph{Setting.}
A T-mesh is a partition of the surface into rectangles, the
\emph{patches}, whose sides are made of \emph{arcs}; nodes where a trace
stops on another are T-junctions, and the singular vertices of the field
are nodes. Each patch $P$ has local coordinates with its first corner at
the origin and its sides along the axes. A quantization assigns every arc
$a$ a length $\ell_a \in \Z_{\ge 0}$ such that opposite sides of every patch
have equal length; the quad mesh is then read off as the integer grid of
each patch.

\begin{definition}[Turn]
A \emph{turn} $\gamma$ is a straight walk across patches, from an offset of
its first side that meets no node: it enters a patch through a side, leaves
through the opposite side at the same distance from the corresponding
corner, enters the neighbouring patch across the arc it reaches, and so on,
until it comes back into its first patch through its first side. The
rotation of the composed crossings is then the identity, since the walk
re-enters its first side inward.
\end{definition}

Crossing from patch $P$ to patch $Q$ across an arc composes a quarter-turn
rotation of the local frame with a translation whose components are signed
sums of arc lengths along the rims of $P$ and $Q$: the position of a rim
point is the sum of the lengths of the arcs between it and the first corner.

\begin{proposition}[The shift of a turn is linear]\label{prop:linear}
For a turn $\gamma$ there are integer coefficients $c_a$, independent of
the lengths, such that after a full turn a point of the first side has
moved across the walking direction by
\[
  \shift_\gamma(\ell) = \sum_a c_a\, \ell_a .
\]
For integer lengths $\ell$, let $o$ be a half-integer offset of the first
side whose walk at the lengths $\ell$ crosses the same patches as $\gamma$
(we call the set of such offsets the \emph{corridor} of $\gamma$ at $\ell$).
Then the dual line of the quad mesh at $o$ comes back at $o +
\shift_\gamma(\ell)$: it closes after one round if and only if
$\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$. If moreover $o + j\,\shift_\gamma(\ell)$ lies in
the corridor for $j = 1, \dots, t - 1$, it winds $t$ times round as a helix of
pitch $|\shift_\gamma(\ell)|$ in the sense of~\cite{bommes2011structure}. If
the corridor holds no half-integer, no strip of the mesh follows $\gamma$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Each crossing is a map $x \mapsto R_k x + t$ with $R_k$ a rotation by $k$
quarter-turns and $t$ a vector whose entries are sums of $\pm\ell_a$. The
composition along $\gamma$ has trivial rotation, hence is a translation
$T_\gamma(\ell)$, linear in $\ell$ with integer coefficients. Its component
along the walking direction does not move a point of the first side off
that side; its component across is $\shift_\gamma(\ell)$. The walk crosses
no singularity (singularities are nodes, never inside a patch), so the
quad mesh is a regular grid along it, and a dual line at distance $o$ from
the first corner, as long as it crosses the same patches as $\gamma$, comes
back at distance $o + \shift_\gamma(\ell)$. The corridor is cut out by two
inequalities per crossing (the walk must cross each arc strictly between its
ends), so it may contain $o$ but not $o + \shift_\gamma(\ell)$: the line then
comes back $\shift_\gamma(\ell)$ rows beside itself, but its next round
follows other patches, and on a closed surface it may close after visiting
them (Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}).
\end{proof}

The shift form is thus a statement about a fixed sequence of crossed
patches. Closing constraints $\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$ describe the strips
of the mesh only together with the corridor inequalities of $\gamma$, which
the engine of Section~\ref{sec:closing} does not impose
(Appendices~\ref{sec:whole} and~\ref{app:endtoend} measure what this costs).
Explicitly, let $\gamma$ cross the arcs $a_1, \dots, a_m$ in turn, and let
$p_j(o, \ell)$ be the distance from the first corner of the side holding
$a_j$ at which the walk from offset $o$ crosses that side. Each crossing map
is an isometry whose translation is an integer form in $\ell$, so
$p_j(o, \ell) = \epsilon_j\, o + q_j(\ell)$ with $\epsilon_j = \pm 1$ and
$q_j$ an integer linear form; let $e_j(\ell)$, a sum of arc lengths, be the
distance of the start of $a_j$ along the same side. The corridor is the
interval
\[
  \bigl\{\, o \;:\; e_j(\ell) < \epsilon_j\, o + q_j(\ell) < e_j(\ell) +
  \ell_{a_j},\ \ j = 1, \dots, m \,\bigr\},
\]
and at whole lengths, for a half-integer $o$, each strict inequality is the
same as one at distance $\tfrac12$ from the ends of the arc:
$e_j(\ell) + \tfrac12 \le \epsilon_j\, o + q_j(\ell) \le e_j(\ell) +
\ell_{a_j} - \tfrac12$. These are the inequalities that
Appendix~\ref{app:endtoend} imposes.

The shift is the across component of the translational holonomy of the
flat metric along the turn (Section~\ref{sec:holonomy}), which is linear in
the lengths as any period of a flat cone
metric~\cite{thurston1998shapes,veech1993flat}, and for a ring of quads it
is the twist of a cylinder. What is specific here is its expression in the
arc lengths of a T-mesh, as a constraint of the quantization.

The component of $T_\gamma(\ell)$ along the walking direction is the
\emph{length} $\alpha_\gamma(\ell)$ of the turn, the distance walked before
coming back to the first side; it is also a linear form in $\ell$ with
integer coefficients, and for whole lengths it is the number of quads of the
strip in one turn.

In openclare (\texttt{quantization/tubes.rs}) the shift is built once per
turn as an expression in the arc lengths, by composing the rim expressions
of the crossed patches; evaluating it with the real lengths of the
parametrization gives the \emph{real shift}, the twist the field itself
would give the cylinder.

\begin{definition}[Strip]
For whole lengths $\ell$ balancing every patch, with every patch side
positive and no two singular vertices at the same point, the quad mesh is,
in each patch, the integer grid of its sides. A \emph{strip} is a maximal sequence
of quads, each sharing with the next the edge opposite the one it shares
with the previous.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Completeness at given lengths]\label{prop:complete}
Let $\ell$ be whole lengths balancing every patch, with every patch side
positive and no two singular vertices at the same point. Every strip of the
quad mesh that crosses a side $\sigma$ of a patch $P$ at the quad edge
$[k, k+1]$ of $\sigma$ is the walk of Definition~1 taken \emph{with the
lengths $\ell$} from $\sigma$ at offset $k + \tfrac12$. In particular, on a
closed surface, walking from every half-integer of every side finds every
strip of the quad mesh: the strips are the cycles of the walk on the
half-integer stations, each oriented strip one cycle; by
Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}, with $\gamma$ the turn the strip follows from
a station, the strip closes after one round exactly when
$\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$. With borders, the strips that reach a border are
open paths of the walk.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Inside $P$, the quads of the strip are the unit squares between the grid
lines at $k$ and $k+1$ across $\sigma$; their dual line is the straight
line at $k + \tfrac12$, which reaches the opposite side at the same distance
from the corresponding corner, which is the move of the walk. At the arc
where it leaves $P$, the crossing map to the neighbouring patch is a
quarter-turn followed by a translation whose components are sums of
$\pm\ell_a$, hence integers: it maps the grid of $P$ onto the grid of the
neighbour, and the midpoint of a quad edge onto the midpoint of a quad
edge. Every node lies at a whole distance along the sides, so a walk at a
half-integer offset never meets a node, and the arc it crosses is
determined. A single arc may have length zero (two T-junctions, or a
T-junction and a corner, at the same point): it holds no half-integer, so no
walk crosses it, and nothing in the argument changes; only the sides of the
patches need to be positive. The strip and the walk thus continue together, patch after
patch. A
strip only meets singular vertices at its sides, never inside, since they
are corners of patches. Positive sides make each patch a grid with at
least one quad in each direction, so that every station lies on a quad edge
of the mesh.
\end{proof}

The sampled walks of Section~\ref{sec:closing} start at fixed shares of
the real side lengths. Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} says where the
helices of a given mesh are \emph{all} found: at the half-integers of the
whole lengths. The engine uses this to generate closing constraints
(Section~\ref{sec:closing}).

\section{Closing turns in quantization}\label{sec:closing}

This section describes the engine at commit \texttt{\enginecommit}, in
\nolinkurl{crates/openclare/src/quantization/}. Some experiments of later
sections used earlier versions; each says which.

The quantizer of openclare minimizes a separable convex cost of the integer
lengths, in the minimum-deviation-flow formulation
of~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf}, subject to the balance of each patch, by
cancelling improving walks in a strip network (a simpler solver than their
matching-based one). Only the structure is theirs: their objective is a sum
of absolute deviations, ours is quadratic. The cost of arc $a$ is
$w_a(\ell_a - \lambda r_a)^2$ with
$w_a = 1/(\lambda r_a + 1)$, plus a term that draws a vertex onto each fold
of the surface the arc crosses, and a penalty for going below a lower bound
(the mean absolute deviation, an $L^1$ measure, is used afterwards, as a cap
in the selection of candidates);
the real lengths $r_a$ carry a fixed per-arc perturbation of at most
$0.5\%$, so that arcs of equal length do not all change at the same scale.
The scale $\lambda$ is searched for the number of quads asked for, and arcs
whose zero length would break the structure of the mesh are held at length
at least one. Closing turns is a side constraint that is not a flow
constraint. We handle it in four steps.

\paragraph{Finding turns.}
From points at given shares along each side of each patch, we walk straight
across patches and keep the walks that return. A turn is \emph{held} if its
real shift is above $0.05$ and its lean below a bound, and if its length is
below a multiple of the side of the T-mesh (the side of a square of its
area). Two turns with the same shift expression up to sign are one ring and
held once. Eight \emph{ways} are tried: starting shares $\{0.5\}$ or $\{0.2,
0.5, 0.8\}$, lean bounds $0.05$ or $0.12$, length bounds $2$ or $6$ sides; a
way that holds the same turns as an earlier one is skipped.

\paragraph{Least-change projection.}
The real lengths $r_a$, at scale one before $\lambda$ is searched, are changed
as little as possible, in the weighted least squares sense, for every held
turn to have zero shift and every patch to stay balanced:
\[
  \min_{x}\ \sum_a v_a\,(x_a - r_a)^2
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad B x = 0,\quad \shift_\gamma(x) = 0\ \ \forall\gamma\in H,
  \qquad v_a = \frac{1}{r_a + 1},
\]
solved with a sparse Cholesky factorization of the balances, computed once
per quantization, and a small dense Schur complement for the turns. Arcs the
solution would make negative are held at zero and the system solved again
(an active set). If the system cannot be met, the held turn left with the
largest shift is let go and the projection repeated. Here $B$ is the matrix
of the balances of the patches; the projected lengths $x$ then take the place
of $r$ in the cost above, and the scale $\lambda$ is searched on them.

\paragraph{Integer search and repair.}
The integer search of the quantizer runs on the projected targets. Rounding
leaves some turns shifted by one or a few rows. Repair walks are then taken
one at a time: a walk is guided by a bias along the arcs of the shifted
turns, and accepted if it reduces the total shift, costs little in
deviation, keeps the structure intact and the number of quads within
tolerance. A second, \emph{aimed} repair treats each held turn still shifted
on its own: the bias is put on its arcs alone, and a walk is accepted if it
shifts that turn less and the others no more. On the pieces that are closed
spheres, walks that cannot close a turn by the parity of
Corollary~\ref{cor:parity}(c) are turned down without being judged, and a
turn whose shift is a non-negative form already at its least
(Remark~\ref{rem:definite}) is not aimed at; the engine also checks, in its
debug builds, the integer coefficients of the shifts, the handshake of
Lemma~\ref{lem:handshake} and the parity of Proposition~\ref{prop:parity}.

\paragraph{Rounds of constraint generation.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} says where the strips of a given mesh are
\emph{all} found: at the half-integers of the whole lengths. After a way
whose lengths are admissible (within the budget and the deviation cap
below), up to three rounds follow, a lazy generation of constraints (the
equations are added as the current solution violates them; they are
equalities on the shifts, not integrality cuts): every
strip of the whole lengths is walked (up to $6$ times the side of the mesh),
those that come back shifted although the real lengths bring them back
within $0.12$ of their length are held as well, and the projection and the
integer search are run again from the lengths of the round before. In these
rounds the projection does not use the active set and the repair is not
aimed. Each round gives one more candidate.

\paragraph{Selection and safeguards.}
For every candidate, the quad mesh connectivity is built and the share of
edges on spiral loops measured (Section~\ref{sec:measure}). A candidate is
kept if it spirals less than the plain search (without closing), its number
of quads is within the tolerance (or no further from the budget than that of
the plain search), its mean deviation from the real lengths
is at most $\max(1.5\,d_0,\ d_0 + 0.2)$ with $d_0$ that of the plain search,
and it leaves the vertices no further from the folds of the surface than the
plain search did, by more than chance would. The three with the fewest
spirals are kept, in that order, then the plain lengths. The ways are
independent and run in parallel. After meshing, if the first candidate
makes a face fold over the surface, the others are meshed in turn until one
does not, and the mesh with the fewest folds, then the fewest spirals, is
kept.

\begin{remark}
A turn round a part of the shape whose real shift is far from zero is not a
rounding artefact: the field winds round the part. We only hold turns whose
real shift is a small share of their length, the \emph{lean}; a turn round
a group of singularities, by contrast, may legitimately come back shifted.
\end{remark}

\section{Measuring loops}\label{sec:measure}

An edge loop is followed straight through every regular vertex (valence
four, interior), each edge belonging to one loop. A loop is
\emph{closed} if it comes back to its start; \emph{border} if it runs from
border to border; \emph{open} if it stops at an irregular vertex or a
non-quad face; and \emph{spiral}, whatever its ends, if a strip of quads
leaving it across meets it again, the same way round, within
$k_{\max} = 4$ quads. The last test catches helices of pitch up to four,
including bundles of interleaved helices~\cite{bommes2011structure}, while a
ring is met again only round a whole torus or over a cap the other way
round. We report the share of edges on each kind of loop, edges of borders
left out.

\section{An exact look at the closing system}\label{sec:exact}

To see how far the projection of Section~\ref{sec:closing} is from what
the constraints allow, an experimental hook of openclare (branch
\texttt{rd-exact}, on an engine before the constraint rounds and the floor of the
deviation cap of Section~\ref{sec:closing}: commit \texttt{3b3122d} and later
are not in it) exports, for each way of finding turns, the system it works
on: the real lengths of the 2{,}069 arcs of the T-mesh
of the male body at 10{,}590 quads, the 1{,}552 balance equations of its
patches, every turn found with its shift, and the lower bounds the integer
search placed on arcs to keep singularities apart (28 to 36 arcs held at
length one). These bounds are the engine's choice of \emph{which} arc of a
chain between two singular vertices carries their separation: validity asks
a chain to keep a positive total length, not a given arc. Every statement
below that uses them is relative to them; we call them the \emph{engine's
bounds of separation}, and $P_{\mathrm{eng}}$ the balanced lengths that meet
them. $P_{\mathrm{eng}}$ is neither a subset nor a superset of the valid
quantizations: it excludes valid lengths that put the separation on another
arc of the chain, and it allows lengths that bring other arcs to zero and
break the mesh. We solve it with HiGHS through SciPy, in real numbers. The ways
are numbered as exported: way $m + 2j + 4i$ starts from the shares $\{0.5\}$
($m = 0$) or $\{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$ ($m = 1$), with the lean bound $0.05$
($j = 0$) or $0.12$ ($j = 1$) and the length bound $2$ ($i = 0$) or $6$
($i = 1$) sides. Way~2 is the one the engine keeps for this body.

\paragraph{Where the conflict lies (measured).}
All held turns can be closed together if the lengths are only asked to be
non-negative. With the bounds of separation, they cannot. In four ways (0,
1, 2 and 4) one held turn cannot even be closed on its own: the same turn
round the left wrist, of length $7.0$, whose shift is a sign-definite form
in two arc lengths (Section~\ref{sec:obstructions},
Remark~\ref{rem:definite}). Giving up the least total amount of the bounds
needed to close all held turns, a linear program, shows that in ways 0, 2
and 4 the bound of a \emph{single} arc stands in the way, the arc of that
wrist turn, $0.08$ quad edges long; Section~\ref{sec:forced} shows that in
ways 0, 2, 4 and 6 letting go of a single turn is enough, and in the others
of four to nine (Table~\ref{tab:forced}). The conflict between closing loops
and keeping singularities apart is thus real, but local.

\paragraph{Whole lengths.}
Appendix~\ref{sec:whole} looks at the same body in whole numbers. The
balances are the incidence matrix of a bidirected graph, whose free arcs
parametrize the lattice of whole balanced lengths
(Proposition~\ref{prop:free}): balances alone never obstruct whole lengths.
Solver failures in whole numbers came from implicit zeros, arcs that every
real solution sets to zero, not from an absence of whole solutions. The
obstruction that remains is the size: closing the $218$ ways round of the
mesh of way~2 that lean like rings needs a mean deviation from the targets
of at least $0.817$, far above the engine's cap; a subset of $190$ closes at
$0.399$ in real numbers and needs at least $0.536$ in whole numbers (proven
by the solver), and the whole lengths found close all $190$ but make a mesh
that spirals more than the engine's, because the strips move with the
lengths. On this body the choice of the turns, not the rounding, decides how
much the mesh spirals.

\section{When rings cannot all close}\label{sec:obstructions}

Section~\ref{sec:exact} found that closing all held turns conflicts with
keeping singularities apart, and that the conflict comes down to one or a
few arcs. This section gives a mechanism for such conflicts, with proofs. The
tool is the holonomy of the flat metric, as used by Izmestiev et
al.~\cite{izmestiev2013torus}; its ingredients are classical, and we know of
no earlier statement of the propositions below in terms of helices.
Appendix~\ref{sec:measured} tests them on the male body, and
Section~\ref{sec:conflicts} describes its conflicts.

\subsection{Turns and singularities}\label{sec:holonomy}

\paragraph{Flat metric and holonomy.}
In Sections~\ref{sec:holonomy} to~\ref{sec:combs} and Appendices~\ref{sec:measured} to~\ref{sec:move} the surface $\Sigma$ is
closed, without border, and \emph{genus zero} means a closed sphere. Several
statements fail with borders or handles; Section~\ref{sec:beyond} gives
their general form.
Lengths $\ell > 0$ balancing every patch make each patch a Euclidean
rectangle. Glued along their arcs, whose lengths agree on both sides, the
rectangles give the surface $\Sigma$ a flat metric with cone points at the
singular vertices: a vertex of valence $k$ has angle $k\pi/2$ and curvature
$(4-k)\pi/2$; a T-junction has angle $2\pi$ and is not a cone point. Let
$\Sigma^\circ$ be $\Sigma$ without its singular vertices, $x_0$ a point inside
a patch, and $R$ the quarter-turn in the positive sense of the orientation of
$\Sigma$. Developing a loop $\gamma$ of $\Sigma^\circ$
based at $x_0$ patch after patch, as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}, gives a motion $\hat h(\gamma)\colon x \mapsto
R^j x + t$ of the coordinates of the first patch, its \emph{holonomy}: the
chart $\varphi$ of $x_0$, continued along $\gamma$, comes back as
$\hat h(\gamma) \circ \varphi$. It
depends only on the homotopy class of $\gamma$ in $\Sigma^\circ$, and $\hat h$
is a homomorphism of the fundamental group into the group of such
motions~\cite{izmestiev2013torus}, $\hat h(\alpha\beta) = \hat h(\alpha) \circ
\hat h(\beta)$. A small loop round a singular vertex $c$
of valence $k$, run with $c$ on its right and reached along a path that
develops it at the point $p$, has
holonomy $x \mapsto R^{4-k}(x - p) + p$: turning clockwise round a cone
point of angle $k\pi/2$ rotates the chart by $-k\pi/2$, which is
$(4-k)\pi/2$ modulo a full turn. When the rotation of $\hat h(\gamma)$
is the identity, its translation is the \emph{Burgers vector} of $\gamma$.

For a turn $\gamma$, let $\bar\gamma$ be the closed curve that follows the
walk and, back in its first patch, returns to its starting point along a
segment parallel to the first side, just inside the patch. Let $e_1$ be the
walking direction and $e_2$ the direction along the first side. By the proof
of Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}, $\hat h(\bar\gamma)$ is a translation and
\[
  \shift_\gamma(\ell) = \langle e_2,\ \hat h(\bar\gamma) \rangle .
\]
Shifts, developed positions and Burgers vectors are linear forms in $\ell$
with integer coefficients, as long as the walks and paths cross the same
patches. An identity between them that holds on an open set of balanced
positive lengths therefore holds for all balanced lengths, and for whole
lengths the developed positions of the nodes are points of $\Z^2$.

\begin{lemma}[Gauss--Bonnet for turns]\label{lem:gauss}
Let $\gamma$ be a turn whose curve $\bar\gamma$ is simple. If $\bar\gamma$
bounds a disc $D \subset \Sigma$, the singular vertices inside $D$ satisfy
$\sum_{c \in D} (4 - k(c)) = 4$. If the curves of two turns are disjoint and
bound an annulus $A$, then $\sum_{c \in A} (4 - k(c)) = 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is the discrete Gauss--Bonnet count, standard in quad
meshing~\cite{bommes2013survey}, applied to the curve of a turn. For a
region of a flat surface with cone points, bounded by straight
segments, Gauss--Bonnet reads $\sum_c \kappa_c + \sum_i \theta_i =
2\pi\chi$, where $\kappa_c$ are the curvatures of the cone points inside and
$\theta_i$ the turning angles at the corners of the border. The curve
$\bar\gamma$ is straight except at the two ends of its closing segment, where
it turns by $+\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$ (or not at all if the shift is zero); the
turning angles sum to zero on each curve, seen from either side. With
$\kappa_c = (4 - k(c))\pi/2$, $\chi(D) = 1$ and $\chi(A) = 0$ the two
statements follow.
\end{proof}

On a closed surface of genus zero, such as a body, every simple closed curve
bounds a disc on each side. Every turn with a simple curve thus splits the
singular vertices into two groups of total index four each, consistently
with the total $8(1-g) = 8$ of Section~\ref{sec:related}: a fingertip beyond
a ring holds the equivalent of four vertices of valence three. A loop round
a group of singularities whose indices do not add up to four is never a
turn, whatever the lengths. A side holding at most one singular vertex has
total index $0$ or $4 - k \neq 4$, so each side of a turn holds at least two.
None of this holds with borders or handles: a side holding $b$ border
components without corners and $h$ handles has total index $4(1 - b - 2h)$, and may hold no
singular vertex at all (Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss-border}).

Curves of turns need not be simple, and on the male body most of those held
for closing are not (Appendix~\ref{sec:measured}). The first case beyond simple
curves is still determined.

\begin{lemma}[One self-crossing]\label{lem:eight}
Let the surface be closed of genus zero, and let the curve $\bar\gamma$ of a turn
cross itself exactly once, transversally, with no singular vertex on it.
It cuts the surface into three discs: two \emph{lobes}, each bounded by one
of the two loops of $\bar\gamma$, and a third region bounded by both. If the
crossing lies on the walk, the totals of $4 - k$ over the singular vertices of
the lobes and the third region are $3$, $3$ and $2$; if it lies on the
closing segment, they are $2$, $2$ and $4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A walk follows the two directions of the field, so it crosses itself at a
right angle. Each loop leaves the crossing point $X$ along one passage and
comes back along the other, so its two rays at $X$ are a quarter-turn apart.
A loop is simple; it bounds on one side a disc containing the quadrant
between its two rays, and the other loop, which meets it only at $X$, lies in
the closure of the other side. That disc is a lobe, with a corner of angle
$\pi/2$ at $X$; the two lobes take opposite quadrants, and the third region
has the other two. The curve is a connected graph on the sphere with one
vertex and two edges, so its three faces are discs. By Gauss--Bonnet as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}, a disc whose border turns by the angles $\theta_i$ at
its corners holds singular vertices of total index $4 - \sum_i
\theta_i/(\pi/2)$. A corner of angle $\pi/2$ turns by $\pi/2$, which gives
$3$, $3$ and $2$ before the corners of the closing segment, at which the
curve turns by $+\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$ in succession. If the crossing lies on
the walk, both of these corners are on one loop, every region sees both, and
they cancel. Otherwise let the walk start at $A$ along $e_1$ and come back at
$Z$, and let it cross the closing segment between them. It crosses walking
along $-e_1$: walking along $e_1$, it would enter the first patch through its
first side there, and the turn would have ended. The loop through $A$ then
turns the same way at $A$ as at $X$, and so does the loop through $Z$ at $Z$
and at $X$ (a direct check of the four directions). Each lobe lies on the
side of its corner of angle $\pi/2$ at $X$, so it sees a second convex
corner and holds $4 - 2 = 2$; the third region holds the rest of the total
$8$, that is $4$.
\end{proof}

With borders or handles the regions need not be discs, and the totals
follow region by region from Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss-border}: turns with one
self-crossing split the singular vertices as $(3, 3, -6)$ on Suzanne,
$(3, 3, -2)$ and $(3, 2, -1)$ on Jerry, and, on the genus-one Bob, into two
regions as $(3, -3)$ and $(2, -2)$ (measured).

\begin{proposition}[The shift is set by the singularities]\label{prop:burgers}
Let $\gamma$, $\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$ be turns with simple curves.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Let $\bar\gamma$ bound a disc $D$ holding the singular vertices
  $c_1, \dots, c_m$, and let $x_0$ be a point of $\bar\gamma$, the start of the
  walk for instance. Let $T \subset D$
  be a tree whose vertices include $c_1, \dots, c_m$ and which meets
  $\bar\gamma$ only at $x_0$. Cut along $T$, the disc $D$ becomes a disc
  $\Omega$ without cone point, which develops into the plane, with the frame
  $(e_1, e_2)$ at $x_0$. Number the $c_i$ in the order in which a walk round
  $T$ from $x_0$, keeping $T$ on its right, first reaches them, and let $p_i$
  be the developed position of $c_i$ at that first visit. Then
  $\shift_\gamma(\ell) = \pm\langle e_2, B_D \rangle$, with
  \[
    B_D = \sum_{i=1}^m Q_i\,(I - R_i)\,p_i ,
    \qquad R_i = R^{4 - k(c_i)},
    \qquad Q_i = R_m R_{m-1} \cdots R_{i+1},
  \]
  the translation of $\rho_m \circ \dots \circ \rho_1$, where $\rho_i(x) =
  R_i(x - p_i) + p_i$; the sign is $+$ if $D$ lies on the right of
  $\bar\gamma$, and $-$ otherwise.
\item[(b)] Let $\bar\gamma_1$ and $\bar\gamma_2$ be disjoint and bound an
  annulus $A$, round which the two turns go the same way, and let a path
  $\kappa$ inside $A$ from $\bar\gamma_1$ to $\bar\gamma_2$ carry the walking
  direction of $\gamma_2$ onto that of $\gamma_1$ (the turns are
  \emph{parallel}); measure both shifts along the direction $e_2$ carried
  by $\kappa$. Then
  \(
    \shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) = \langle e_2, B_A \rangle,
  \)
  where $B_A$ is the Burgers vector of the border of the disc $A$ cut along
  $\kappa$, given by the same sum over the singular vertices inside $A$,
  developed inside the cut disc.
\item[(c)] In particular, parallel turns bounding an annulus without
  singular vertex have the same shift for every $\ell$: a helix is a
  property of a whole cylinder of parallel turns, not of one of them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Orient each edge $f$ of $T$ away from $x_0$. Its two sides on the border
of $\Omega$ are glued by a motion $G_f$: a point of $f$ that develops at $u$
on the side the walk round $T$ follows first develops at $G_f(u)$ on the
other. Let $f$ end at the vertex $v$, from which the edges $f_1, \dots, f_r$
leave in the order of the walk. Keeping $T$ on its right, the walk turns
clockwise round $v$, so a small arc round $v$ from the first side of $f$ to
the second turns clockwise through the whole angle at $v$ and crosses $f_1,
\dots, f_r$ on its way. Continuing the chart of $\Omega$ along this arc
composes it with $G_{f_1}^{-1}, \dots, G_{f_r}^{-1}$ at the crossings, and
turning once round $v$ composes it with the rotation $\rho_v(x) =
R^{4-k(v)}(x - p_v) + p_v$, where $p_v$ is the position of $v$ at the first
visit, the one the arc starts from. Comparing the two at the second side of
$f$,
\[
  G_f = G_{f_r} \circ \dots \circ G_{f_1} \circ \rho_v ,
\]
with $\rho_v$ the identity if $v$ is not singular. The same comparison at
$x_0$, with the edges $f_1, \dots, f_r$ leaving $x_0$ and the arc round $v$
replaced by $\bar\gamma$ run with $D$ on its right, gives $\hat
h(\bar\gamma) = G_{f_r} \circ \dots \circ G_{f_1}$. Unfolding the recursion,
$\hat h(\bar\gamma)$ is the composition of the $\rho_{c_i}$, each vertex
before the vertices below it and the branches in the order of the walk: the
order of first visit. Its rotation is $R^{\sum_i(4 - k(c_i))} = R^4 = I$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}, and expanding the composition gives $B_D$; run with
$D$ on its left, $\bar\gamma$ has the inverse holonomy, the translation by
$-B_D$. The shift is the component along $e_2$. When $T$ is a star of
disjoint paths from $x_0$, the order is the one in which the paths leave
$x_0$, as the walk meets them.
(b) Take $x_0$ on $\bar\gamma_1$ at the start of $\kappa$. The loop $\lambda
= \bar\gamma_1\, \kappa\, \bar\gamma_2^{-1} \kappa^{-1}$ is the border of
the disc obtained by cutting $A$ along $\kappa$. Developing along $\kappa$ is
a change of coordinates; conjugating the translation $\hat h(\bar\gamma_2)$
by it gives that translation read in the coordinates at $x_0$, where the walk
of $\gamma_2$ runs along $e_1$ by hypothesis. Hence $\hat h(\lambda)$ is the
translation by $\hat h(\bar\gamma_1) - \hat h(\bar\gamma_2)$, both read in
the same coordinates, whose component along $e_2$ is the difference of the
shifts, and part~(a) applied to this disc, whose singular
vertices have total index zero by Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}, gives the sum.
(c) Without singular vertex the sum is empty.
\end{proof}

The simplest configuration between two parallel turns is a pair of
singular vertices of valences three and five, the defect that lets the number
of edges round a limb change. It is a dislocation of the grid. That a pair
of opposite disclinations is a dislocation, with a Burgers vector of length
$2\sin(\Omega/2)$ times their separation for a rotation $\Omega$, is
classical in the theory of crystal
defects~\cite[Eq.~(5)]{kleman2008disclinations}; on a quad mesh $\Omega =
\pi/2$, and on a triangle mesh a pair of valences five and seven is the
elementary dislocation of graphene~\cite{yazyev2010graphene}. What follows
reads it on the shifts of two turns.

\begin{proposition}[A dislocation between two turns]\label{prop:dislocation}
In the setting of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b), let the annulus $A$ hold
exactly two singular vertices, $c_3$ of valence three and $c_5$ of valence
five, and let $d = p_5 - p_3 = d_1 e_1 + d_2 e_2$ be their separation,
developed inside the cut disc. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $\shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) =
  \varepsilon_1 d_1 + \varepsilon_2 d_2$ with signs $\varepsilon_1,
  \varepsilon_2 \in \{+1, -1\}$ that depend on the configuration but not on
  $\ell$, and $|B_A| = \sqrt2\,|d|$. The two turns can close together only
  if $c_5$ lies, as seen from $c_3$, on one particular diagonal of the field.
\item[(ii)] If $c_3$ and $c_5$ lie on one straight line of the field inside
  $A$, of length $\delta$ (in a T-mesh: they are joined by a chain of arcs
  along one trace, and $\delta$ is the sum of their lengths), then
  $|\shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell)| = \delta$. The two
  turns can both be rings only if $\delta = 0$, that is if the two
  singularities merge; as long as the bounds of separation keep
  $\delta \ge 1$, one of them at least is a helix.
\item[(iii)] For whole lengths, $\shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) -
  \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) \equiv d_1 + d_2 \pmod 2$: a pair at an odd
  number of edges from one another, counted along the two directions of the
  field, never lies between two rings.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $R_3 = R$ and $R_5 = R^{-1}$, the sum of
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers} for the two possible orders is
$B_A = R^{-1}(I - R)\,p_3 + (I - R^{-1})\,p_5 = (I - R^{-1})\,d$ or
$B_A = R\,(I - R^{-1})\,p_5 + (I - R)\,p_3 = (I - R)(-d)$. In coordinates
$(e_1, e_2)$, $R(a, b) = (-b, a)$, so
$(I - R^{-1})(d_1, d_2) = (d_1 - d_2,\ d_1 + d_2)$ and
$(I - R)(-d_1, -d_2) = (-d_1 - d_2,\ d_1 - d_2)$; reversing the
orientation of the border, which inverts the rotations, gives the two other
sign patterns. (We checked the
four cases symbolically.) In all of them both components of $B_A$ are of
the form $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$ and $|B_A|^2 = 2|d|^2$, which is (i); the
component along $e_2$ vanishes exactly on the line $\varepsilon_1 d_1 +
\varepsilon_2 d_2 = 0$, a diagonal. For (ii), choose $\kappa$ away from the
straight segment joining $c_3$ to $c_5$, which does not separate the two
borders of $A$, take the tree of part~(a) to contain that segment so that
the positions of~(i) are those of the first visit, and develop $c_5$ from
$c_3$ along the segment: $d$ is $\delta$ times
one of $\pm e_1$, $\pm e_2$, so $|\varepsilon_1 d_1 + \varepsilon_2 d_2| =
\delta$. For (iii), $d \in \Z^2$ and $\pm d_1 \pm d_2 \equiv d_1 + d_2$.
\end{proof}

Plain counting confirms (iii) independently. If both turns were rings, the
along component of $B_A$ would be the difference $n_1 - n_2$ of the numbers
of edges round them, of the parity of $d_1 + d_2$; but an annulus made of
quads has an even number of edges on its border, since $4F = 2E_{\text{inside}}
+ E_{\text{border}}$, so $n_1 + n_2$ is even. Proposition~\ref{prop:parity}
below makes this a statement about each turn. Part (ii) is the mechanism the
problem of feasibility in Section~\ref{sec:open} guessed, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase} extends it from straight chains to
staircases. It does not explain the conflict found on the male body at a
single arc of the left wrist (Section~\ref{sec:exact}): that conflict
involves a single turn, not two (Remark~\ref{rem:definite}), and
Proposition~\ref{prop:winding} gives its mechanism.

For more singular vertices between two turns, the parity part generalizes
into a checkerboard rule.

\begin{proposition}[Checkerboard rule]\label{prop:checkerboard}
In the setting of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b), with whole lengths,
colour the points $p \in \Z^2$ of the cut disc black or white by the parity
of $p_1 + p_2$ (either colour may be called white). Then $\shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell)$ has
the parity of the number of singular vertices of odd valence inside $A$
that develop onto white points. In particular, two rings bound an annulus
only if that number is even.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the sum of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}, a vertex of even valence has
$R_i = I$ or $R_i = -I$, and $(I - R_i)p_i \in \{0, 2p_i\}$ is even. A vertex
of odd valence has $R_i = R^{\pm1}$, and both components of $(I - R^{\pm1})
(a, b) = (a \pm b,\ b \mp a)$ have the parity of $a + b$. Quarter-turns keep
the property ``both components have the parity $\sigma$'', and sums add the
$\sigma$. So both components of $B_A$ have the parity of the number of odd
vertices on white points. The count does not depend on the coordinates: a
quarter-turn keeps colours, and a whole translation $t$ changes the colour of
every point if $t_1 + t_2$ is odd, which keeps the parity of the count
because the number of odd vertices inside $A$ is even, their indices
$4 - k$ being odd and summing to zero with the even ones
(Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}); for the same reason the two colours give counts of
the same parity. Developing a vertex along another path inside the
cut disc moves it by a rotation $x \mapsto R_j(x - p_j) + p_j$ about another
singular vertex, which keeps its colour.
\end{proof}
We also checked the rule on $13{,}000$ random configurations with SymPy.

\paragraph{Parity on a sphere.}
The checkerboard rule compares two turns. On a closed surface of genus zero
each turn carries its own parity condition, which needs neither a simple curve
nor the holonomy. Let $B$ be the matrix of the balances of the patches, so
that the balanced lengths are the solutions of $B\ell = 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Parity of a turn on a sphere]\label{prop:parity}
Let the surface be closed of genus zero and the T-mesh admit lengths that are
positive and balance every patch (its real lengths do). For every turn
$\gamma$ and all whole lengths $\ell$ balancing every patch, of any sign,
\[
  \alpha_\gamma(\ell) \equiv \shift_\gamma(\ell) \pmod 2 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First let $\ell$ be whole, balanced, and positive on every arc. The patches
are then grids of unit squares glued along their arcs, a quad mesh of the
sphere. Its graph is bipartite: over $\Z/2$, the cycles of a graph embedded
in a sphere are sums of face boundaries, since $H_1(S^2; \Z/2) = 0$, and every
face boundary has four edges, so every cycle has an even length. Fix a
colouring of the vertices in two colours, adjacent vertices differing. In
each patch $P$ the grid is connected, so the colour of its point of local
coordinates $(i, j)$ is $i + j + \kappa_P \bmod 2$ for a constant $\kappa_P$.
For a motion $M(x) = R^k x + t$ with $t \in \Z^2$, write $\pi(M) = t_1 + t_2
\bmod 2$; since a quarter-turn keeps the parity of $i + j$, the sum of the
coordinates of $M(x)$ has the parity of that of $x$ plus $\pi(M)$.

Let $P_0, P_1, \dots, P_N = P_0$ be the patches crossed by $\gamma$ and $D_k$
the motion that develops the coordinates of $P_k$ into those of $P_0$, as in
the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}; $D_0$ is the identity and $D_N$
the translation $T_\gamma(\ell)$. We show $\kappa_{P_k} \equiv \kappa_{P_0} +
\pi(D_k)$ by induction. A vertex $w$ of the arc crossed from $P_k$ to
$P_{k+1}$ has coordinates $y$ in $P_k$ and $y'$ in $P_{k+1}$ with $D_k(y) =
D_{k+1}(y')$; its colour is $y_1 + y_2 + \kappa_{P_k} \equiv (D_k y)_1 + (D_k
y)_2 + \kappa_{P_0}$ by the induction hypothesis, and also $y'_1 + y'_2 +
\kappa_{P_{k+1}}$; as $(D_{k+1} y')_1 + (D_{k+1} y')_2 \equiv y'_1 + y'_2 +
\pi(D_{k+1})$, this gives $\kappa_{P_{k+1}} \equiv \kappa_{P_0} +
\pi(D_{k+1})$. For $k = N$ the patch is $P_0$ again, so $\pi(T_\gamma(\ell))
\equiv 0$. In the frame $(e_1, e_2)$ of the first side, which is that of $P_0$
up to a quarter-turn and signs (the coordinates swap when the first side is
along the first axis of $P_0$), $T_\gamma(\ell) = (\alpha_\gamma(\ell),
\pm\shift_\gamma(\ell))$; a quarter-turn keeps the parity of the sum of the
coordinates, hence $\alpha_\gamma(\ell) + \shift_\gamma(\ell)$ is even.

For general whole balanced $\ell$: the balanced lengths form a rational
subspace, positive real balanced lengths exist, and positivity is an open
condition, so there are positive rational balanced lengths, and, multiplying
by a common denominator, positive whole ones $p$. For $N$ large, $\ell + Np$
is whole, balanced and positive, and $\alpha_\gamma + \shift_\gamma$, being
linear, takes at $\ell = (\ell + Np) - Np$ the difference of two even values.
\end{proof}

The proof uses only that the quad graph is bipartite, so it fails in higher
genus: on a torus, a $3 \times 3$ grid of quads has rings of three quads; and
with borders (Theorem~\ref{thm:parity-any}). On a closed sphere it gives at
once:

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:parity}
On a closed surface of genus zero:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] every ring of quads has an even number of quads;
\item[(b)] a helix of odd pitch winds round an odd number of quads per turn;
\item[(c)] closing a helix of odd pitch changes the length of its walk by an
  odd number of edges: a repair that keeps the length of a turn cannot close
  it if its pitch is odd, and changing the pitch by an odd amount always
  changes the length.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(a) By Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} a ring is the strip of a walk with
$\shift = 0$, so $\alpha$ is even. (b) and (c) are the proposition read
before and after the change.
\end{proof}

Part (a) also follows directly from the bipartite graph: either border of a
ring is a cycle of the quad graph with as many edges as the ring has quads.
It is the classical bipartiteness of quadrangulations of the sphere; more
generally, homologous cycles of a quadrangulation have the same
parity~\cite[Lemma~3.1]{kaiser2015colouring}.
The proposition is consistent with the earlier statements and refines them.
For a turn whose curve bounds a disc $D$, the argument of the checkerboard
rule applied to Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a) shows that both components
of $B_D = (\alpha_\gamma, \shift_\gamma)$ have the parity of the number of
vertices of odd valence of $D$ on white points: they are equal, which is
Proposition~\ref{prop:parity} for that turn. For two parallel turns, the
proposition applied to each gives $\shift_{\gamma_1} - \shift_{\gamma_2} \equiv
\alpha_{\gamma_1} - \alpha_{\gamma_2}$, the two components of $B_A$, which the
checkerboard rule and Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(iii) evaluate from
the singularities: two rings ($\shift_{\gamma_1} = \shift_{\gamma_2} = 0$) have
even lengths, so no 3--5 pair at odd separation, and no odd number of odd
vertices on white points, lies between them. On the eight systems exported for
the male body (Section~\ref{sec:exact}), the proposition holds on all 1{,}838
ways round found by walking every strip of the meshes (measured).

\begin{remark}[Borders; a second proof]\label{rem:borders}
The proposition and part (a) of the corollary need the surface to be closed:
on a sphere with two border loops, an open cylinder of three quads round has
a ring of three quads. The proof uses $H_1(S^2; \Z/2) = 0$, which borders
break. On a closed sphere there is a second proof, which needs neither the
bipartite graph nor positive lengths, and which the formalization of
Section~\ref{sec:lean} found. Every loop of $\Sigma^\circ$ is a product of
the loops $\lambda_i$ round the singular vertices (Section~\ref{sec:fox}),
whose holonomies are quarter-turns about the points $p_i$. For whole lengths
these are points of $\Z^2$, and a quarter-turn about a whole point keeps the
parity of $p_1 + p_2$. So the holonomy of every loop keeps that parity, and
its translation, the image of the origin, has an even sum of coordinates:
for a turn, $\alpha_\gamma + \shift_\gamma$ is even.
Theorem~\ref{thm:parity-any} gives the rule on any surface, and the eye
sockets of Suzanne a case met in practice: a ring of $23$ quads.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{How many independent rings.}
Rings that cross neither one another nor themselves are disjoint simple
closed curves of
$\Sigma^\circ$, none bounding a disc with fewer than two singular vertices
(Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}). On a closed surface of genus zero with $n \ge 3$
singular vertices there are at most $n - 3$ such curves pairwise non-homotopic, the
number of curves of a decomposition of the $n$-punctured sphere into pairs of
pants, the classical bound on the number of cylinders of a Jenkins--Strebel
differential~\cite{strebel1984quadratic}; disjoint homotopic ones bound an annulus without singular vertex and
share their shift by Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(c). The rings of a
quad mesh that do not cross therefore fall into at most $n - 3$ cylinders,
the cylinders of the Jenkins--Strebel picture, and their closing conditions
have rank at most $n - 3$. On a surface of genus $g$ with $b$ border
components the count of a decomposition into pairs of pants is
$3g - 3 + n + b$, plus at most $b$ classes of collars parallel to the
borders (Proposition~\ref{prop:collar}); a torus without singular vertex has
one class.

\begin{remark}[Whole periods are automatic; rings need zero]
For whole lengths, every shift is a whole number: the period of the integer
grid along a loop is always whole, which is what quantization guarantees and
what~\cite{kalberer2011stripe} enforces by rounding. Closing a ring is the
stronger requirement that this period be zero. The real shift of the
parametrization is the twist of the field's cylinder; it tells which whole
value rounding is likely to pick, not whether zero is reachable.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Sign-definite turns; Farkas certificates]\label{rem:definite}
Say that a linear form $f$ in the lengths equals $g$ \emph{modulo the
balances} if $f - g$ is a combination of the rows of $B$, so that $f(\ell) =
g(\ell)$ for every balanced $\ell$. If the shift of a turn equals, modulo the
balances, a form $\sum_a c_a \ell_a$ with every $c_a \ge 0$, then for all
balanced $\ell \ge 0$ the shift $\shift_\gamma(\ell)$ is never negative,
and it is zero only if every arc with $c_a > 0$ has length zero. If one
of those arcs is held open by a bound of separation, the turn is a helix under
every quantization meeting the bounds; it should never be held, and one linear
program per turn finds such turns. More generally, by Farkas' lemma (with
$x = b + z$, $z \ge 0$), the system $B\ell = 0$, $\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$ for
$\gamma \in H$, $\ell \ge b$ has no solution exactly when some combination
$\sum_{\gamma \in H} u_\gamma \shift_\gamma$ equals, modulo the balances, a form
$\sum_a c_a \ell_a$ with $c \ge 0$ and $\sum_a c_a b_a > 0$; and an arc $a$
has length zero in every solution with $\ell \ge 0$ exactly when some such
combination has $c_a > 0$. The data being integral, bounds included, the
multipliers of the shifts and of the balances can be taken whole.
\end{remark}

On the male body this is what happens at the left wrist (measured; the
certificate checked in exact rational arithmetic for way 0): in ways 0, 1, 2
and 4 one held turn, of length $7.0$,
has a shift equal, modulo the balances, to $-(\ell_{1921} + \ell_{1937})$,
where arc 1937, $0.08$ edges long, is held at length at least one. That turn
cannot be closed on its own, within the engine's bounds of separation.
Whether $\ell_{1937} = 0$ would merge two singular vertices, that is whether
every valid quantization meets this bound, we did not check.
Section~\ref{sec:conflicts} shows where this form comes from.

\begin{remark}[Whole solutions exist at some size]\label{rem:exist}
The balance and closing constraints are homogeneous and the bounds of
separation are lower bounds $\ell \ge b$ with $b \ge 0$. If they have a
real solution they have a rational one, a vertex of a rational polyhedron,
and multiplying it by the common denominator $N$ gives whole lengths
$N\ell \ge Nb \ge b$ that satisfy them all. Closing in whole numbers is thus
never impossible in itself; what can fail is closing \emph{at a given size}
(number of quads) or within a given deviation from the targets.
Appendix~\ref{sec:whole} measures this on the male body.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{On the male body.}
Appendix~\ref{sec:measured} tests the statements above on the T-mesh of the
male body exported in Section~\ref{sec:exact}: they hold on every turn and
annulus they apply to. It also shows that most of the turns held for closing
have curves that cross themselves, so that the statements on simple curves
say nothing about them; Section~\ref{sec:fox} handles any curve.

The smallest counterexample has three turns, in way 1 near the right eye,
of lengths $54.9$, $52.8$ and $108.1$:
\[
  \shift_{36} + \shift_{37} - \shift_{39} = \ell_{604} + \ell_{619} + \ell_{620}

\]
with $\ell_{619} \ge 1$ imposed, so no lengths meeting the bounds close all
three, while every two of them close together in whole numbers. Sets of four
and seven turns behave alike, with the same signature: a combination of the
shifts equals a non-negative form containing a bounded arc. Every set found
is infeasible in real numbers already: none of these obstructions is one of
parity. In the conflicts between two turns, the sum or difference of the
shifts equals a sum of two to eight arc lengths with coefficient one, one of
them bounded; the two turns have nearly equal lengths ($39.7$ and $40.0$,
$52.4$ and $52.8$, $277.5$ and $278.0$, \dots) and lie in clusters of
singularities, at the eyes, the hands and the feet. One may read this as
Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(ii): a vertex of valence three and
one of valence five joined along one trace by a chain holding the bounded
arc. The T-mesh refutes that reading: we examined $13$ conflicts between two turns
(every row of two turns in Table~\ref{tab:iis} except the pair $49$, $71$ of
way~5), and in none of them are the turns separated by such a pair on one
trace. What separates them is a staircase.

\begin{proposition}[Staircases]\label{prop:staircase}
Let $\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$ be turns and $c$, $c'$ singular vertices such that
\[
  \shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) = \varepsilon_1 d_1 +
  \varepsilon_2 d_2
\]
for all balanced $\ell$, with $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \in \{+1, -1\}$
and $d = p_{c'} - p_c = d_1 e_1 + d_2 e_2$ the separation of $c'$ from $c$
developed along a path $\pi$ from $c$ to $c'$
(Propositions~\ref{prop:dislocation}(i) and~\ref{prop:swap} give such
identities). Let $\sigma$ be a path from $c$ to $c'$ along arcs $a_1, \dots,
a_r$, through no other singular vertex, and homotopic to $\pi$ with its ends
fixed in $\Sigma$ without the other singular vertices. Developed along
$\sigma$, each arc $a_i$ runs along a direction $v_i \in \{\pm e_1, \pm
e_2\}$. If $\varepsilon_1 v_{i,1} + \varepsilon_2 v_{i,2} = 1$ for every $i$,
so that $\sigma$ is a \emph{staircase}, using two directions a quarter-turn
apart and climbing across the diagonal on which the two turns could close,
then
\[
  \shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) = \sum_{i=1}^r \ell_{a_i}
\]
for all balanced $\ell$. Both turns are rings only if every arc of $\sigma$
has length zero. If this sum equals, modulo the balances, a form with
non-negative coefficients containing an arc held at length at least one, one
of the two turns is a helix under every quantization meeting the bounds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Developing along $\sigma$ adds, arc after arc, the vector $\ell_{a_i} v_i$:
the nodes $\sigma$ passes are T-junctions, where the flat metric has no cone
point. As $\sigma$ and $\pi$ are homotopic with fixed ends away from the
other cone points, they develop $c'$ at the same place relative to $c$, so
$d = \sum_i \ell_{a_i} v_i$ and $\varepsilon_1 d_1 + \varepsilon_2 d_2 =
\sum_i \ell_{a_i}$, first for positive balanced lengths, then for all by
linearity. If both shifts vanish, this sum of non-negative lengths is zero.
\end{proof}

The proof uses only $\varepsilon_1 v_{i,1} + \varepsilon_2 v_{i,2} = 1$ for
each step: neither that the steps run along the axes nor that $\varepsilon_1,
\varepsilon_2 = \pm1$, so it applies to any path on whose steps the linear
form of the shift difference is one.

Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(ii) is the case of a straight $\sigma$,
along one direction. The second source of such identities is an exchange of
two vertices of equal valence.

\begin{proposition}[Exchanging two vertices]\label{prop:swap}
Let $\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$ be turns, and $\kappa$ a path from the start of
$\bar\gamma_1$ to that of $\bar\gamma_2$ that carries the walking direction
of $\gamma_2$ onto that of $\gamma_1$, as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b); let $\lambda = \bar\gamma_1\, \kappa\,
\bar\gamma_2^{-1} \kappa^{-1}$. Suppose that $\lambda$ is homotopic in
$\Sigma^\circ$ to $\lambda_c \lambda_{c'}^{-1}$, where $\lambda_c$ and
$\lambda_{c'}$ are small loops run with $c$, $c'$ on their right round two
singular vertices of the same valence $k$, reached along paths that develop
them at $p_c$ and $p_{c'}$. Then
\[
  \shift_{\gamma_1}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_2}(\ell) = \bigl\langle e_2,\
  (I - R^{4-k})(p_c - p_{c'}) \bigr\rangle .
\]
For valence three or five this is $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$ with $d = p_c - p_{c'}$,
as for a dislocation, and Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase} applies.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
As in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b), $\hat h(\lambda)$ is
the translation by $\hat h(\bar\gamma_1) - \hat h(\bar\gamma_2)$, read in the
coordinates at the start of $\gamma_1$. With $j = 4 - k$,
\[
  \hat h(\lambda_c) \circ \hat h(\lambda_{c'})^{-1}(x) =
  R^j\bigl(R^{-j}(x - p_{c'}) + p_{c'} - p_c\bigr) + p_c
  = x + (I - R^j)(p_c - p_{c'}),
\]
and the shifts are the components along $e_2$. For $j = \pm1$ both
components of $(I - R^j)d$ are of the form $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$, as in the proof
of Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{A separatrix that winds.}
The one turn that cannot be closed on its own is of another kind.

\begin{proposition}[A separatrix that winds]\label{prop:winding}
Let $\sigma$ and $\tau$ be separatrices of a singular vertex $v$ such that
$\sigma$, followed from $v$, stops on $\tau$ at a point $q$ at distance
$\delta(\ell)$ from $v$ along $\tau$, a sum of arc lengths. Let $\lambda$ be
the loop that runs along $\sigma$ from $v$ to $q$ and back along $\tau$ to
$v$, pushed off $v$ into the sector it turns through. If $\lambda$ is freely
homotopic in $\Sigma^\circ$ to the curve $\bar\gamma$ of a turn $\gamma$, the
walking direction of $\gamma$ going to that of $\sigma$, then
$\shift_\gamma(\ell) = \pm\delta(\ell)$ for all balanced $\ell$. The turn is
a ring only if $\delta = 0$, that is if $\sigma$ comes back onto its own
vertex; if $\delta$ equals, modulo the balances, a non-negative form
containing an arc held at length at least one, $\gamma$ is a helix under
every quantization meeting the bounds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Develop $\lambda$ from a point of $\sigma$ next to $v$, with $v$ at the
origin and $\sigma$ along $e_1$. The separatrix $\sigma$ is straight and
stops on $\tau$ at a right angle, so the return along $\tau$ is a segment of
length $\delta$ along $\pm e_2$, like the closing segment of a turn. Being
homotopic to the curve of a turn, $\lambda$ has a translation as holonomy;
it maps $v$, developed at the start, onto $v$ developed at the end, which
lies at distance $\delta$ from $q$ across $\sigma$. Its component across is
thus $\pm\delta$. A free homotopy conjugates the holonomy by a motion; the
conjugate of a translation is the translation by the rotated vector, and the
rotation is the identity since it carries the walking direction of $\gamma$
onto that of $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

At the left wrist the held turn (42 in way 0, length $6.99$, real shift
$-0.305$, whole shift $-1$) is simple, and its small side holds four
vertices of valence three, $226$, $243$, $244$ and $245$, of total index
$4$: a cap. Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a) on that side gives the shift
$-0.305$ from their four positions, but as a form in the lengths of the arcs
inside the cap it has no sign. The sign comes from outside. The vertex $246$,
of valence five, lies beyond the cap; one of its separatrices, the trace
$964$, runs $0.066 + 6.928 = 6.994$ edges once round the wrist, parallel to
the turn, and stops on another of its separatrices, the trace $966$, at the
T-junction $1099$, at the distance $\delta = \ell_{1920} + \ell_{1921} = 0.075
+ 0.230 = 0.305$ from $246$. The shift of the turn is $-\delta$ modulo the
balances, and $\ell_{1920}$ equals, modulo the balances, the length of arc
$1937$, which starts at the vertex $248$ of valence five and is held at
length at least one: whence the certificate $-(\ell_{1921} + \ell_{1937})$ of
Remark~\ref{rem:definite}, and $\shift \le -1$ at every admissible whole
length (measured). A ring would need the separatrix to come back onto its
own vertex, which the engine's bounds of separation forbid. As a check, the
along component of the holonomy is the length of $\sigma$, $6.994$, and
that of the turn is $6.99$. We checked the identity,
not the homotopy that Proposition~\ref{prop:winding} assumes.

\paragraph{Conflicts of more than two turns.}
The three turns at the right eye are a dislocation between three turns. The
turns $36$ and $37$ are simple; $39$ is a figure-eight; each two of them
cross twice, and together they cut the surface into five regions. The cycle
$\bar\gamma_{36} + \bar\gamma_{37} - \bar\gamma_{39}$ winds $-1$ round the
one region bordered by all three curves, which holds the vertices $79$ of
valence three and $77$ of valence five, and round no other singular vertex:
in homology, $39$ is the sum of $36$ and $37$ and a loop round the pair. The
combination $\shift_{36} + \shift_{37} - \shift_{39}$ is the length of a
staircase of three arcs from $79$ to $77$ that includes the held arc $619$,
$1.367 = 1.712 - 0.955 + 0.610$ at the real lengths (measured). The set of
four turns at the eyes ($40$, $56$, $59$, $61$ of way 1, $72$ to $167$ long,
crossing themselves up to $17$ times and each other up to $28$ times) winds
$+1$ round the region holding $198$ (valence three) and $199$ (valence five),
$-1$ round $207$ and $+1$ round $202$ (both valence three), and round no
other singular vertex: in homology, a dislocation and an exchange, the held
arc $1587$ starting at $199$; a bounded search did not write the combination
as a sum of two staircases, and a floating-point test of
Section~\ref{sec:combs} separates its certificate from the non-negative
combinations of staircases, caps and winding separatrices.

The fractional multipliers are an artefact of normalization. The linear
program that finds the certificate asks the bounded arcs to weigh one in
total: at the left hand the form has three bounded arcs of coefficient
$\tfrac13$ in way 1 and two of coefficient $\tfrac12$ in way 3. Scaled to
whole numbers, both ways give the same primitive multipliers,
\[
  7\,\shift_{66} + 5\,\shift_{71} + 2\,\shift_{75} + 3\,\shift_{76}
  = -2F \quad\text{modulo the balances},
\]
with $F$ a form of non-negative whole coefficients $1$, $2$ and $5$
containing the bounded arcs (measured). With these weights the weighted
cycle winds alike round the rest of the body ($236$ singular vertices) and
round the region of ten singular vertices of total index $-2$.
Section~\ref{sec:fox} explains part of these weights: why $-2F$ is even, and
which dislocation carries the coefficient $5$.

\begin{question}[Are conflicts round a cluster?]\label{conj:region}
On a closed surface of genus zero, let $H$ be an irreducible set of turns that
cannot be closed together within the bounds of separation. Are the curves
of the turns of $H$, up to the cylinders of
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(c), the $k \ge 1$ borders of one planar region
round a cluster of singularities, and is a combination of their shifts that
certifies the conflict given by the holonomy relation of these borders: a
form with non-negative coefficients containing an arc held open?
\end{question}

\emph{Answer: no.} The certificates alone suggest it, and the T-mesh
confirms it for one turn (the wrist, a cap,
though the form comes from a separatrix outside the region) and for the
annulus of configuration~A. It holds in a modified form for the three turns at
the right eye: the curves are not the borders of a pair of pants, $39$ being
a figure-eight, but their combination winds round one region holding one
pair of valences three and five, and the certificate is the staircase of
that pair. As stated, it is refuted by the conflicts made of crossing turns:
configurations B to F, and the sets of four and seven turns, whose curves
cross each other up to tens of times and bound no single planar region. What
the confirmed cases share is local, which suggests a refined question.

\begin{question}[Are conflicts sums of local dislocations?]\label{conj:local}
On a closed surface of genus zero, let $H$ be an irreducible set of turns that
cannot be closed together within the bounds of separation. Does some
combination $\sum_{\gamma \in H} u_\gamma \shift_\gamma$ with whole
multipliers that certifies the conflict (Remark~\ref{rem:definite}) equal,
modulo the balances, a sum with non-negative whole weights of
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] lengths of staircases (Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase}) between a
  vertex of valence three and one of valence five, or between two vertices of
  equal valence exchanged as in Proposition~\ref{prop:swap}, and
\item[(ii)] shifts of caps: sign-definite forms carried by curves that wind
  once round singular vertices of total index four, as the wrist turn and its
  separatrix (Proposition~\ref{prop:winding}),
\end{enumerate}
one term at least containing an arc held open?
\end{question}

\emph{Answer: no as stated, yes and empty when read loosely.} It holds, with
a single term and up to the homotopies we did not check, for the wrist,
configurations A to D and the three turns at the right eye.
Section~\ref{sec:combs} settles it on the male body: read strictly, with
staircases of weight one, monotone caps and winding separatrices only, it
is false (configurations E and F, in exact arithmetic); read loosely, with
every local dislocation allowed, it is true for every certificate and says
nothing that Farkas' lemma does not.

\subsection{Any combination of turns}\label{sec:fox}

The conflicts above were described by the winding numbers of a weighted
cycle $\sum_\gamma u_\gamma \bar\gamma$. Winding numbers are its class in the
homology of $\Sigma^\circ$, which is commutative, while the holonomy is not; so
they cannot carry the whole holonomy, and Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}
needed an order of first visit. The bookkeeping that keeps the order is the
free differential calculus of Fox~\cite{fox1953free}. It turns
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers} into a formula for any combination of turns.

\paragraph{Setting.}
Let $\Sigma$ be closed of genus zero, with singular vertices $c_1, \dots, c_n$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers-any} adds the terms of borders and handles). Fix a
point $x_0$ inside a patch, with the frame of that patch, and disjoint paths
$\pi_i$ from $x_0$ to $c_i$; let $\lambda_i$ be the loop that follows $\pi_i$,
goes round $c_i$ with $c_i$ on its right, and comes back. The fundamental group
of $\Sigma^\circ$ is generated by the $\lambda_i$, with a single relation, the
product of all of them in the order in which the paths leave $x_0$. Let $p_i$
be the developed position of $c_i$ at the end of $\pi_i$ and $R_i =
R^{4 - k(c_i)}$, so that $\hat h(\lambda_i) = \rho_i$, $\rho_i(x) = R_i(x - p_i)
+ p_i$. For a loop $\gamma$ at $x_0$ write $\hat h(\gamma)(x) = \rho(\gamma)x
+ \tau(\gamma)$; since $\hat h$ is a homomorphism,
$\tau(\alpha\beta) = \tau(\alpha) + \rho(\alpha)\,\tau(\beta)$. Let $F$ be the
free group on letters $x_1, \dots, x_n$, mapped onto the fundamental group by
$x_i \mapsto \lambda_i$, and $\partial/\partial x_i$ the Fox derivatives of its
group ring: $\partial x_j/\partial x_i = \delta_{ij}$ and $\partial(uv)/\partial
x_i = \partial u/\partial x_i + u\,\partial v/\partial x_i$. Extend $\rho$
linearly to the group ring; its values are the integer matrices $a_0 I + a_1 R
+ a_2 R^2 + a_3 R^3$, and the augmentation $\epsilon$ maps such an element to
$a_0 + a_1 + a_2 + a_3$, the sum of the coefficients of the group ring element.

The first identity below is the defining property of the Fox derivatives
for a crossed homomorphism of the free group~\cite{fox1953free}, applied to
the translation part of the holonomy, with $\tau(\lambda_i) = (I -
R_i)p_i$; the developed positions of the cone points are, up to a common
motion, the twisted-cohomology coordinates of the flat
metric~\cite{thurston1998shapes,veech1993flat}. What is new is its reading
on combinations of turns.

\begin{proposition}[Fox--Burgers formula]\label{prop:fox}
For every word $w$ of $F$,
\[
  \tau(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n \rho\Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)
  (I - R_i)\,p_i .
\]
Let $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_m$ be turns, $\kappa_r$ a path from $x_0$ to the
start of $\bar\gamma_r$, $f_r$ the direction $e_2$ of $\gamma_r$ carried back
to $x_0$ along $\kappa_r$ (one of $\pm e_1$, $\pm e_2$), and $w_r$ a word for
$\kappa_r \bar\gamma_r \kappa_r^{-1}$. For multipliers $u_r$ define the
\emph{twisted winding} of the weighted cycle round $c_i$,
\[
  W_i = \sum_{r=1}^m u_r\, \rho\Bigl(\frac{\partial w_r}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)^{\!\top} f_r
  \in \Z^2 \text{ (for whole } u_r) .
\]
Then, for all balanced lengths,
\[
  \sum_{r=1}^m u_r\, \shift_{\gamma_r}(\ell) = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl\langle W_i,\
  (I - R_i)\,p_i \bigr\rangle ,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^n (I - R_i)^\top W_i = 0 ,
\]
so the sum does not change when all the $p_i$ are moved by one translation:
it is a combination of separations $p_i - p_j$. The ordinary winding number of
$\sum_r u_r \bar\gamma_r$ round $c_i$ is, up to a constant common to all $i$,
$w_i = \sum_r u_r\, \epsilon(\partial w_r/\partial x_i)$. The words $w_r$
are defined up to the relation, and the $W_i$ accordingly: inserting a
conjugate $g\varrho g^{-1}$ of the relation $\varrho$ in $w_r$, after a
prefix $a$ of $w_r$, adds $u_r \rho(a g\, \partial \varrho/\partial x_i)^\top
f_r$ to every $W_i$ (with $a$ empty, $u_r \rho(g\, \partial
\varrho/\partial x_i)^\top f_r$), and leaves the sum unchanged.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The map $w \mapsto \tau(w)$ and the right-hand side are both maps $F \to
\R^2$ with $\phi(uv) = \phi(u) + \rho(u)\phi(v)$: the first by the rule above,
the second by the rule of the Fox derivatives and the multiplicativity of
$\rho$. They agree on the letters, where $\partial x_j/\partial x_i =
\delta_{ij}$ and $\tau(\lambda_i) = (I - R_i)p_i$, and on the empty word, so
they agree everywhere by induction on the length of words (for $x_i^{-1}$, use
$0 = \phi(x_i x_i^{-1})$). For a turn, $\hat h(\kappa_r \bar\gamma_r
\kappa_r^{-1})$ is the conjugate of the translation $\hat h(\bar\gamma_r)$ by
$\hat h(\kappa_r)$, the translation by $M_r T_r$, where $T_r$ is the
translation of $\bar\gamma_r$ in its own frame and $M_r$ the rotation of $\hat
h(\kappa_r)$. Hence $\shift_{\gamma_r} = \langle e_2, T_r \rangle = \langle
M_r e_2, M_r T_r \rangle = \langle f_r, \tau(w_r) \rangle$, and $\langle f,
\rho(D)\, b\rangle = \langle \rho(D)^\top f, b \rangle$ gives the formula. The
fundamental formula of Fox calculus, $\sum_i (\partial w/\partial x_i)(x_i - 1)
= w - 1$, maps under $\rho$ to $\sum_i \rho(\partial w_r/\partial x_i)(R_i - I)
= \rho(w_r) - I = 0$, the rotation of a turn being trivial; transposed and
summed with the $u_r f_r$, it is the second identity. Finally
$\epsilon(\partial w/\partial x_i)$ is the exponent sum of $x_i$ in $w$, which
is the class of $w$ in the homology of $\Sigma^\circ$ written on the basis of
the $\lambda_i$, defined up to the relation, in which each $\lambda_i$ appears
once: up to a common constant, the winding numbers.
\end{proof}

For a simple turn the word is a product of conjugates of the $x_i$, each
once. Written as the composition $\rho_m \circ \dots \circ \rho_1$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a), $c_m$ on the left and $c_1$ on the right,
the derivative $\partial w/\partial x_i$ is the prefix left of $x_i$, so that
$\rho(\partial w/\partial x_i) = Q_i = R_m \cdots R_{i+1}$, and the formula is
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a). In general the twisted winding $W_i$ is
an integer vector, not a number: the cycle may go round $c_i$ several times,
each time with another rotation before it. Reduced modulo $2$, the rotations
disappear.

\begin{corollary}[Checkerboard rule for any combination]\label{cor:checker}
On a closed surface of genus zero, let $\ell$ be whole and balanced and the
multipliers $u_r$ whole. Colour each singular vertex $c_i$ by the parity
$\chi_i$ of $p_{i,1} + p_{i,2}$; it does not depend on the paths $\pi_i$, and
for positive lengths it is the colour of $c_i$ in the bipartition of the quad
mesh. Then
\[
  \sum_r u_r\, \shift_{\gamma_r}(\ell) \equiv \sum_r u_r\, \alpha_{\gamma_r}(\ell)
  \equiv \sum_{i:\ k(c_i) \text{ odd}} w_i\, \chi_i \pmod 2 ,
\]
with $w_i$ the winding numbers of $\sum_r u_r \bar\gamma_r$, whatever the
common constant and whatever the orientation of each $\bar\gamma_r$. In
particular, a combination whose cycle winds an even number of times round
every vertex of odd valence takes even values at every whole length.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $\rho(D) = \sum_k a_k R^k$, with $\sum_k a_k = \epsilon(D)$. Since $(R -
I)^2 = -2R$, $R^k(I - R^{\pm1}) \equiv I - R^{\pm1}$ modulo $2$, so $\rho(D)(I
- R_i) \equiv \epsilon(D)(I - R_i)$ for $k(c_i)$ odd, and $\rho(D)(I - R_i)
\equiv 0$ for $k(c_i)$ even, where $I - R_i \in \{0, 2I\}$. For $f \in \{\pm
e_1, \pm e_2\}$ and $p \in \Z^2$, $\langle f, (I - R^{\pm1})p\rangle \equiv p_1
+ p_2$. This gives the value modulo $2$ of the sum of
Proposition~\ref{prop:fox}, and the same computation with the direction $e_1$
of each turn gives that of $\sum_r u_r \alpha_{\gamma_r}$. A rotation about a
singular vertex at a whole point, or a quarter-turn, keeps the parity of
$p_1 + p_2$, so $\chi_i$ does not depend on the paths; a whole translation of
the origin flips all colours, which adds $\sum_{k(c_i)\text{ odd}} w_i$, even
because the rotation of the cycle is trivial ($\sum_i w_i (4 - k(c_i))
\equiv 0 \pmod 4$). Adding a constant $q$ to all $w_i$ adds $q
\sum_{k(c_i)\text{ odd}} \chi_i$, which is even: it is the value modulo $2$
of the relation, a loop with zero translation. Reversing a turn changes the
sign of its winding numbers, which does not matter modulo $2$. For whole
lengths with every patch side positive the developed parity is a proper
colouring of the quad graph,
as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:parity}.
\end{proof}

The corollary contains the checkerboard rule of
Proposition~\ref{prop:checkerboard} (winding one inside the annulus, zero
outside), Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(iii), and, on a sphere,
Proposition~\ref{prop:parity}: for a single turn $\shift$ and $\alpha$ have the
same parity because both are congruent to the same sum. With borders or
handles the colours may depend on the paths: the count of vertices on white
points still gives the parity when every curve lies in a planar region
without border, and in general $\sum_r u_r(\alpha_{\gamma_r} +
\shift_{\gamma_r}) \equiv \sum_r u_r\, \Omega_\ell([\bar\gamma_r])$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:parity-any}).

As in the theory of crystal defects, whose group of defects is not
commutative~\cite{kleman2008disclinations}, and as in the commutator
argument of Izmestiev et al.~\cite[\S7]{izmestiev2013torus}, winding numbers
do not determine the holonomy:

\begin{proposition}[Winding numbers do not suffice]\label{prop:commutator}
Let $c_a$, $c_b$ be singular vertices of odd valence developed at distinct
points $p_a \neq p_b$. The loop $\lambda_a \lambda_b \lambda_a^{-1}
\lambda_b^{-1}$ winds zero times round every singular vertex, and its holonomy
is the translation by $(I - R_a)(I - R_b)(p_a - p_b)$, of length $2|p_a -
p_b| \neq 0$. No function of the winding numbers gives the holonomy of the
loops with trivial rotation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $w = x_a x_b x_a^{-1} x_b^{-1}$, $\partial w/\partial x_a = 1 - x_a x_b
x_a^{-1}$ and $\partial w/\partial x_b = x_a - w$, whose images under $\rho$
are $I - R_b$ and $R_a - I$, the rotations commuting. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:fox}, $\tau(w) = (I - R_b)(I - R_a)p_a + (R_a - I)(I -
R_b)p_b$. For odd valences $I - R_a$ and $I - R_b$ are $\sqrt2$ times a
rotation. The exponent sums of $w$ are zero.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Measured on the male body.}
We implemented Proposition~\ref{prop:fox} on the T-mesh of
Appendix~\ref{sec:measured}. The patches are developed along a breadth-first
tree of patches; the arcs this tree does not cross form a spanning tree of the
$1{,}295$ nodes, and the surface cut along it is a disc with one chart.
Crossing an arc of the tree changes the chart by a motion; rooted at a singular
vertex, every such motion is the composition, in the cyclic order round the
node, of the motions of the arcs below it and of the rotation about the node,
the recursion in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a). It holds at
every node of the tree with one convention of order, found among eight, the
places $p_i$ being corners of the nodes in the chart. The holonomy of every
turn is then a word in the rotations, and the formula of
Proposition~\ref{prop:fox} gives its translation, in both components,
for all $1{,}299$ turns of the eight ways (identities modulo the balances;
measured throughout this paragraph). Corollary~\ref{cor:checker} holds, at the
whole lengths of the engine, on all $1{,}299$ turns, $718$ simple, $101$ with
one self-crossing and $480$ with more, and on the $14$ infeasible sets of
Table~\ref{tab:iis} with whole multipliers (all but the set of fifteen). For
the set at the left hand, with multipliers $(7, 5, 2, 3)$, the cycle winds an
even number of times round every vertex of odd valence: the corollary is why
its certificate $-2F$ is even at every whole length.

Proposition~\ref{prop:commutator} is not idle on this body: the turns $18$ of
way~0 ($7$ self-crossings), $22$ of way~0 (simple), $101$ of way~4 ($4$) and
$65$ of way~5 ($17$) satisfy $w_{18} = w_{22} + w_{101} + w_{65}$ exactly,
round every singular vertex, while no choice of quarter-turn frames makes
their translations satisfy the same relation (the smallest residual has norm
$0.25$ on an orthonormal basis of the balanced lengths). A ``winding Burgers
formula'' with numbers for weights is thus false, on the body as in principle;
it holds modulo $2$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:checker}), and over the reals with the
twisted windings.

\subsection{Combs}\label{sec:combs}

The twisted windings of Proposition~\ref{prop:fox} depend on the chart, so
grouping them into clusters is not a statement about the surface. Another
reading of a certificate is intrinsic, and turns every arc into a local
dislocation.

\paragraph{Feet and combs.}
In the T-meshes of openclare, traced from the separatrices of the singular
vertices only, every trace of a closed surface starts at a singular vertex
(feature lines or seeded traces would break this; we assume it below). For
an arc $a$, along the direction $e_a$ from the node $n_0$ to the node $n_1$,
call \emph{foot} of $n_j$ the node itself if it is singular, and otherwise the
singular vertex where the trace through $n_j$ perpendicular to $a$ starts. The
\emph{comb} $\sigma_a$ of $a$ is the path from the foot $c_0$ of $n_0$ along
its trace to $n_0$, along $a$ to $n_1$, and along the other trace to the foot
$c_1$ of $n_1$; it passes through T-junctions only. Call \emph{graph of feet}
the graph whose vertices are the singular vertices of odd valence and whose
edges are the arcs whose two feet are distinct vertices of odd valence.

\begin{lemma}[Combs]\label{lem:comb}
Let $p_{c_0}$, $p_{c_1}$ be the feet of an arc $a$ developed along its comb.
Then, for all balanced lengths,
\[
  \langle e_a,\ p_{c_1} - p_{c_0} \rangle = \ell_a .
\]
If $c_0 \neq c_1$ have odd valence, $2\ell_a$ is, up to sign, the form of the
twisted class $\lambda_{c_0}\lambda_{c_1}^{\pm1} \otimes v$ of the loops round
the two feet reached along the comb, for a whole $v$ with $(I - R^{\pm1})^\top
v = 2e_a$: the pair is a dislocation if the valences differ
(Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}) and an exchange if they are equal
(Proposition~\ref{prop:swap}). If $c_0 = c_1$, $\ell_a$ is the shift of a
winding separatrix (Proposition~\ref{prop:winding}).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Developed along the comb, every step but $a$ runs along a trace perpendicular
to $a$ and through no cone point, so it adds a vector orthogonal to $e_a$; the
step along $a$ adds $\ell_a e_a$. For the pair, the loop $\lambda_{c_0}
\lambda_{c_1}^{\pm1}$, with the sign that makes its rotation trivial for
valences three and five and the inverse for equal valences, has the translation
$\pm(I - R^{\pm1})(p_{c_0} - p_{c_1})$, as in the proofs of
Propositions~\ref{prop:dislocation} and~\ref{prop:swap}; pairing it with $v$
gives $\pm\langle (I - R^{\pm1})^\top v, p_{c_0} - p_{c_1}\rangle = \pm 2\ell_a$.
Such a $v$ is whole because $I - R^{\pm1}$ maps $\Z^2$ onto the lattice $L$ of
vectors whose two coordinates have the same parity, which contains $2e_a$. If
$c_0 = c_1$, the two traces are separatrices of one vertex, the second
stopping on the first at the distance $\ell_a$, the setting of
Proposition~\ref{prop:winding}.
\end{proof}

A \emph{closed walk of combs} is a sequence of arcs $a_1, \dots, a_k$ whose
feet are $c_{i-1}$ and $c_i$, of odd valence, with $c_k = c_0$; its
\emph{reading} is $\sum_i \ell_{a_i}$. A walk of length one is a winding
separatrix, a walk of length two between a vertex of valence three and one of
valence five the staircase of Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase}, and a walk
round four vertices of valence three a cap.

\begin{proposition}[Certificates are sums of combs]\label{thm:combs}
Let $c = \sum_a c_a\ell_a$, $c_a \in \Z_{\ge0}$, be a certificate
(Remark~\ref{rem:definite}) whose arcs have feet of odd valence and whose odd
part, the arcs with $c_a$ odd, is a cycle modulo~$2$ of the graph of feet.
Then $c$ is a sum with whole non-negative weights of comb readings $2\ell_a$,
each the form of a dislocation or an exchange (Lemma~\ref{lem:comb}), and of
readings of closed walks of combs. With real weights, every non-negative form
supported on arcs whose two feet are of odd valence, distinct or equal, is a
non-negative combination of comb readings $2\ell_a$ and of winding
separatrices $\ell_a$, without condition of parity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The odd part, a cycle modulo $2$, splits into closed walks along its edges
(Veblen); what remains is $\sum_a k_a\,2\ell_a$ with $k_a = \lfloor c_a/2
\rfloor$. With real weights, $c = \sum_a \tfrac{c_a}{2}\,(2\ell_a)$ on the
arcs with two distinct feet, and $c_a\,\ell_a$, a walk of length one, on the
arcs whose two feet are one vertex.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Closed walks as twisted cycles]
We expect the reading of a closed walk of combs to be the Burgers form of a
twisted cycle supported near its combs: by Lemma~\ref{lem:comb} and a
summation by parts the reading is $\sum_i \langle e_{a_i} - e_{a_{i+1}},\
p_{c_i}\rangle$, and two unit vectors differ by an element of the image of
$(I - R_{c_i})^\top$. But each $p_{c_i}$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:comb} is developed
along its own comb, and the two combs through $c_i$ develop it in charts that
differ by the rotation of the sector between them; a proof must develop the
whole walk in one chart, insert these rotations, and account for the
holonomy of the loop of the walk at its closing. We have not written it, and
we use closed walks below only as forms in the lengths.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The Burgers pairing]\label{prop:pairing}
Let the surface be closed of genus zero, with no singular vertex of valence
divisible by four, and let every trace of the T-mesh start at a singular
vertex. Then the map that sends a class of the twisted homology
$H_1(\Sigma^\circ; \R^2_\rho)$ to its form $\sum_i \langle W_i, (I - R_i)p_i
\rangle$ on the balanced lengths is onto the dual of the balanced lengths. Its
kernel contains the class across each separatrix connection. If some
singular vertices have valence divisible by four, no form depends on their
developed positions, and the map need not be onto.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If every such form vanishes at balanced lengths $\ell$, every loop of
$\Sigma^\circ$ with trivial rotation has a holonomy without translation, since
the components of its translation are such forms
(Proposition~\ref{prop:fox}). For two singular vertices $a$, $b$, the
commutator of $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_b$ has trivial rotation, the rotations
commuting, and by the computation of Proposition~\ref{prop:commutator} its
translation is $(I - R_a)(I - R_b)(p_a - p_b)$. No $R_i$ is the identity, so
$I - R_i$ is invertible ($I - R^{\pm1}$ is $\sqrt2$ times a rotation, $I - R^2
= 2I$), and $p_a = p_b$: every singular vertex develops at one point $q$.
Every $\rho_i$ fixes $q$; on a closed sphere the $\lambda_i$ generate the
fundamental group of $\Sigma^\circ$, so every holonomy fixes $q$, and
developed along any path every singular vertex lands at $q$. Every arc has
feet, since every trace starts at a singular vertex, so every comb offset of
Lemma~\ref{lem:comb} is zero, and $\ell = 0$. The image of the map is thus a
subspace of the dual with no common zero but $0$: the whole dual. Across a
connection, a separatrix joins two singular vertices, whose separation along
it is the length of the connection in the direction of the trace and zero
across; the twisted class pairing the across direction with it has the zero
form. A vertex of valence divisible by four has $R_i = I$, so $(I - R_i)p_i =
0$ and no form depends on $p_i$: its developed position is a relative period,
which deformations of the lengths can move without changing the holonomy of
any loop of $\Sigma^\circ$.
\end{proof}

The pairing is the T-mesh form of a classical fact: the developed positions
of the cone points are local coordinates of flat cone metrics, as twisted
(co)homology classes~\cite{thurston1998shapes,veech1993flat}.

\paragraph{Measured on the male body.}
The T-mesh has $1{,}035$ traces; $1{,}034$ end at T-junctions and one is a
separatrix connection, from vertex $204$ to vertex $210$. Lemma~\ref{lem:comb}
holds on all $2{,}069$ arcs. Of them, $2{,}046$ have two distinct feet of odd
valence ($691$ of valences three and five, $450$ of valences three and three,
$905$ of valences five and five), $14$ have a single foot of valence five,

has rank $517$, the dimension of the balanced lengths, and its kernel is the

singular vertex of valence divisible by four the kernel is spanned by the
classes across the connections. All $25$ certificates of the
conflicts examined have a representative, modulo the balances, whose odd part

to all of them. The wrist certificate is, modulo the balances, the reading
$\ell_{1902}$ of a comb whose two feet are the vertex $246$: the winding
separatrix. Configuration~A is a closed walk of two combs between $76$ and
$78$, the staircase $\ell_{611} + \ell_{624}$; C and D are walks of two
combs, between $109$ and $118$ and between $155$ and $156$; B a walk of four
combs through $102$, $129$, $103$ and $105$; the three turns at the right eye
a walk of three through $76$, $77$ and $79$. Configuration~E is a closed walk
of six combs through $198$, $199$, $204$, $208$, $207$ and $209$, of total
index $-2$; F a walk of four combs through the vertices $14$, $24$, $23$ and
$15$, all of valence three: a cap. The left hand is five times the

\paragraph{Question~\ref{conj:local}, read loosely, is true and empty.}
Allowing every local dislocation and exchange, that is every comb reading
$2\ell_a$ and every closed walk of combs, Proposition~\ref{thm:combs} proves the
conjecture for every certificate whose odd part is a cycle of the graph of
feet, which is all those of the male body. But it follows from Farkas' lemma
and a parity: nearly every arc is a comb, so every non-negative form on arcs
whose feet are of odd valence is a sum of local dislocations, and the
conjecture in this reading says nothing about which configurations of
singularities conflict.

\paragraph{Read strictly, it is false.}
Restricted to staircases of weight one between vertices of odd valence,
monotone closed walks round a cap and winding separatrices, as
Question~\ref{conj:local} states it, the certificates of configurations E and F are not
non-negative combinations modulo the balances: exact rational balanced
lengths make every such form non-negative and the certificate negative,
$-2.9967$ for E and $-1.9973$ for F (measured, checked in integer
arithmetic). These forms are infinitely many, but each is the length of a
walk on a graph whose steps carry the arc lengths, so a negative one, if any,
is a negative cycle or path, which a shortest-path search finds: the
separation covers them all. The same test in floating point separates the set of four turns
at the eyes (optimum $-2$, over $3{,}146$ generators; not checked exactly)
and finds the left hand inside the cone; the sets of seven and fifteen were
not tested. The test is membership of the certificate in the cone of these
local forms modulo the balances; that an extreme ray of the cone of
certificates is neither a staircase nor a cap would not refute anything.
Whether every certificate splits into combs on disjoint sets of vertices, each
of the sign of the certificate, is open: the trees of the left hand and of
way~7 share vertices between their combs.

Appendix~\ref{sec:move} tests a lever before quantization suggested by
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}, moving the singular vertices in the
parametrization: in a map of fixed scale it barely moves the shifts.

\subsection{Borders and handles}\label{sec:beyond}

The bench holds models with borders (the eye sockets of Suzanne, the mouth of
Jerry) and with handles (Bob, a torus; part and cage, of higher genus). This
subsection gives the general form of the statements that need a closed sphere,
and says which hold unchanged. A border node $b$ is a corner of the flat
metric of angle $a(b)\pi/2$; its \emph{index} is $2 - a(b)$, zero on a straight
border. For a border component $\beta_j$ and lengths $\ell$, let $L_j(\ell)$ be
the sum of the lengths of its arcs.

\begin{lemma}[Gauss--Bonnet with borders]\label{lem:gauss-border}
Let $D$ be a region bounded by curves of turns and by border components. Then
\[
  \sum_{c \in D} (4 - k(c)) + \sum_{b \in D \cap \partial\Sigma} (2 - a(b))
  = 4\chi(D) - \Theta,
\]
where $\Theta$ is the turning of the curves of the turns at their corners, in
quarter-turns: zero for the side of a simple curve.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Gauss--Bonnet for a flat surface with cone points and a piecewise straight
boundary; border nodes are corners of the boundary, of turning $(2 - a)\pi/2$.
\end{proof}
A side of a simple turn holding $b$ border components without corner and $h$
handles has index $4(1 - b - 2h)$: with a border it may hold no singular
vertex at all. The lemma holds on the $1{,}076$ sides of turns of Suzanne and
Jerry; the sides that are not discs have total index $0$ (one border) or $-4$
(two borders), $40$ of them collars round an eye socket with no singular
vertex; on Bob those holding the handle have $-4$, on part $-4$ and $-12$
(measured).

For positive lengths the homomorphism of the next theorem is the cycle
parity of the quad graph, a $\Z/2$-homology invariant of any
quadrangulation~\cite[Lemma~3.1]{kaiser2015colouring} (see
also~\cite{archdeacon2001chromatic}); the theorem extends it to the shifts
and lengths of turns at all balanced lengths, borders included.

\begin{theorem}[Parity of a turn on any surface]\label{thm:parity-any}
Let $\ell$ be whole and balanced. There is a homomorphism
$\Omega_\ell\colon H_1(\Sigma; \Z/2) \to \Z/2$ such that for every turn
\[
  \alpha_\gamma(\ell) + \shift_\gamma(\ell) \equiv \Omega_\ell([\bar\gamma]) \pmod 2 .
\]
Its value on a border component is the parity of $L_j(\ell)$, the sum of all
$L_j(\ell)$ is even, and for positive lengths $\Omega_\ell(z)$ is the parity of
the length of any cycle of the quad graph in the class $z$; $\Omega_\ell = 0$
exactly when the quad graph is bipartite. On a surface of genus zero with
borders, $\alpha_\gamma + \shift_\gamma$ has the parity of the total length of
the border components on either side of $\bar\gamma$, or of the number of
times $\bar\gamma$ crosses any system of disjoint arcs joining the border
components of odd length in pairs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a motion $M(x) = R^k x + t$ with $t$ whole, the colour $p_1 + p_2 \bmod 2$
of $M(x)$ is that of $x$ plus $\pi(M) = t_1 + t_2 \bmod 2$, so $\pi$ is a
homomorphism, and so is $\gamma \mapsto \pi(\hat h(\gamma))$. A loop round a
singular vertex is a quarter-turn about a whole point, which keeps colours: it
has $\pi = 0$. The values being in a group of exponent two, the map factors
through $H_1(\Sigma^\circ; \Z/2)$, and through $H_1(\Sigma; \Z/2)$ since the
loops round the singular vertices span the kernel of $H_1(\Sigma^\circ) \to
H_1(\Sigma)$. For a turn, $\hat h(\bar\gamma)$ is the translation
$(\alpha_\gamma, \pm\shift_\gamma)$ up to a quarter-turn. Developed from one of
its nodes, a whole point, a border component comes back displaced by
$\sum_a \pm\ell_a v_a$ with $v_a$ unit vectors of the axes, whose colour is
that of $L_j(\ell)$. The border components add up to the boundary of $\Sigma$,
zero in homology. Along a cycle of the quad graph every edge changes the
colour. On a surface of genus zero the classes of the border components span
$H_1(\Sigma; \Z/2)$ and $\bar\gamma$ is homologous to those on one of its
sides; the last form is Lefschetz duality.
\end{proof}

On the head of Suzanne, whose eye sockets are borders of $23$ and $25$ edges,
the turn round an eye socket is a ring of $23$ quads at the lengths of one way
and of $24$ at those of another: Proposition~\ref{prop:parity} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:parity}(a) and~(c) fail there. Its shift is zero for every
balanced length, since a border without corner is a closed straight line of
the flat metric:

\begin{proposition}[Collars]\label{prop:collar}
Let the curve of a turn $\gamma$ and a border component $\beta_j$ without
corner bound an annulus holding no singular vertex. Then
$\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$ and $\alpha_\gamma(\ell) = L_j(\ell)$ for all
balanced lengths.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A border without corner is straight, so its holonomy is the translation by
$L_j$ along its direction, an axis of the field. Cut along a path $\kappa$
from $\bar\gamma$ to $\beta_j$, the annulus is a disc without cone point,
whose border has trivial holonomy, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b):
the translation of $\bar\gamma$ is that of $\beta_j$ carried along $\kappa$,
the vector $L_j u$ with $u$ one of $\pm e_1$, $\pm e_2$. For positive lengths
its component along the walk, $\alpha_\gamma$, is positive, so $u = \pm e_1$:
the shift is zero and $\alpha_\gamma = L_j$; both identities are linear and
extend to all balanced lengths.
\end{proof}

The theorem predicts the parity on all $660$ turns of Suzanne, Jerry and a
dome, $61$ of them with $\alpha + \shift$ odd, and the proposition holds on
all $40$ collars (measured). On the output meshes, every closed loop has the
parity the theorem gives: $36$ of $36$ on Suzanne, $5$ of $5$ on Jerry, $15$ of
$15$ on a tube, $40$ of $40$ on a cylinder, $14$ of $14$ on Bob and $294$ of
$294$ on the male body. In higher genus, the turns null in $H_1(\Sigma;\Z/2)$
all have $\alpha + \shift$ even ($120$ on Bob, $50$ on part, $4$ on cage, $30$
on a cylinder), the parity is constant on homology classes and additive, and
$\Omega_\ell$ depends on the lengths: the three ways of Bob give it the values
$(0, 1)$, $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ on two handle loops, and $22$ of the $80$
handle loops of one of its meshes have odd length (measured).

\begin{proposition}[Burgers sum with borders and handles]\label{prop:burgers-any}
Let the loops $\lambda_i$ round the singular vertices, $\beta_j$ round the
border components and $a_k, b_k$ round the handles generate the fundamental
group of $\Sigma^\circ$. For every word $w$ in them,
\[
  \tau(w) = \sum_i \rho\Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)(I - R_i)\,p_i
  + \sum_{g \in \{\beta_j, a_k, b_k\}} \rho\Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial g}\Bigr)\tau(g).
\]
For a turn, the shift is the Burgers sum of the singular vertices plus a
\emph{period term}, linear in the lengths and not determined by the developed
positions of the singular vertices. A border component without corner has
holonomy the translation by $L_j$ along its developed direction; a handle
inside the region between two turns contributes the commutator
$(I - \rho(b))\tau(a) - (I - \rho(a))\tau(b)$, zero when both its loops have
trivial rotation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Both sides are maps $\phi$ with $\phi(uv) = \phi(u) + \rho(u)\phi(v)$ that agree
on the generators, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:fox}. The
commutator of $x \mapsto Ax + a$ and $x \mapsto Bx + b$ with commuting
rotations is the translation by $(I - B)a - (I - A)b$.
\end{proof}

With its border term, the sum reproduces the translation of all $314$ sides
holding a border on which we tested it; without it, it reproduces none of the
$586$ such sides of Suzanne and Jerry (measured). Sides that are discs follow Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a)
verbatim ($86$ of $86$ on Bob, $25$ of $25$ on part); on a torus without
singular vertex the shifts are $\pm\ell_0$ or $\pm\ell_2$, pure periods
(measured). With the period term, Remark~\ref{rem:definite}, the staircases,
Theorem~\ref{thm:forced} and Corollary~\ref{cor:chain}, which use no topology,
hold on any surface; when the twisted windings of the handle loops vanish, a
combination of shifts is again a form in the developed positions of the
singular vertices alone.

\begin{proposition}[Two crossing turns on a torus]\label{prop:torus}
On a closed surface of genus one, let the curves of two turns be simple and
cross once. Cut along both, the surface is a disc $D$ holding every singular
vertex, and its Burgers sum vanishes: $\sum_i Q_i (I - R_i) p_i = 0$, in the
order of first visit, for all balanced lengths. More generally, if $\kappa$ is
a simple loop crossing the curve of a turn once, with rotation $R^j$, then
$(I - R^j)\,T_\gamma = \pm B_D$: for $j \neq 0$ the translation of the turn is
set by the singular vertices, for $j = 0$ it is a free period and the singular
vertices satisfy $B_D = 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The border of $D$ is the commutator of the two curves, whose holonomies are
translations; their commutator is the identity, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a) applies to the disc $D$. With $\kappa$ of
rotation $R^j$, the commutator is the translation by $(I - R^j)T_\gamma$.
\end{proof}

On the T-mesh of Bob, of genus one with ten vertices of valence three and ten
of valence five, the Burgers sum of the twenty vertices vanishes in all $304$
pairs of simple turns crossing once, and in none of $90$ trials with the order
shuffled (measured). With a single pair of valences three and five the sum is
$(I - R^{\pm1})(p_5 - p_3)$: a torus with two turns crossing once must develop
the two cone points onto one point. That no such torus exists at all is the
theorem of Barnette, Jucovi\v{c} and
Trenkler~\cite{barnette1971toroidal}; it
also follows from Abel's theorem, the quartic differential $(dz)^4$ of the
flat metric having the divisor $c_5 - c_3$, which is principal on an elliptic
curve only if $c_5 = c_3$. The obstruction is to existence, not to closing.
Proposition~\ref{prop:torus} with $j = 0$ is the commutator argument
discussed by Izmestiev et al.~\cite[\S7]{izmestiev2013torus}: it excludes a
3,5-quadrangulation of the torus with two turns crossing once, but not one
whose handle loops rotate, which their Holonomy Theorem handles. The conflict actually met on Bob is of another
kind: two turns that wind three and two times round the same handle, whose
shifts carry its period with the coefficients $3$ and $2$; the combination
$3\shift_{33} - 2\shift_{31}$ cancels the period and equals, modulo the
balances, the sum of two arc lengths at a vertex of valence three, one of
them held open (measured). On Bob, each of the $102$ held turns closes on
its own, and all of them close together in five of the six ways (measured).

\section{Turns every quantization within bounds leaves shifted}\label{sec:forced}

The obstructions of Section~\ref{sec:obstructions} say that a set of turns
cannot close together. Turned around, they bound from below the number of
turns of a given family that any quantization in a set $P$ of lengths leaves
shifted, and so tell how far a heuristic such as the one of
Section~\ref{sec:closing} is from the best possible within $P$. On the male
body, $P$ is defined by the engine's bounds of separation
(Section~\ref{sec:exact}), so the measured bounds are relative to them, not
to the singularity configuration alone. We are not aware of an earlier lower
bound of this kind for helices (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\begin{theorem}[Forced shifts]\label{thm:forced}
Let $H$ be a finite set of turns of a T-mesh and $P$ a non-empty set of
lengths containing every quantization considered: for instance the balanced
lengths meeting given bounds of separation, $\ell \ge b$, possibly with a cap
$\frac1n \sum_a |\ell_a - t_a| \le D$ on the mean deviation from the targets
$t$, or with a given number of quads. Let
\[
  \tau_P(H) = \min\bigl\{\, |X| : X \subseteq H,\ \text{the turns of } H
  \setminus X \text{ can be closed together by lengths in } P \,\bigr\}.
\]
Then for every $\ell \in P$, whole or not, at least $\tau_P(H)$ turns of $H$
have $\shift_\gamma(\ell) \neq 0$. If $H$ contains $k$ pairwise disjoint subsets
none of which can be closed within $P$, then $\tau_P(H) \ge k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At any $\ell \in P$, whole or not, the turns of $H$ with zero shift are closed
by $\ell$ itself, which lies in $P$; so the shifted turns form a set $X$ as
in the definition, and $|X| \ge \tau_P(H)$. A set $X$ whose removal leaves a
closable set meets each of the $k$ subsets, which are disjoint, so $|X| \ge
k$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is elementary; its content is in what makes subsets infeasible,
which Section~\ref{sec:obstructions} relates to the singularities. The proof
uses neither convexity, nor linearity of the shifts, nor whole lengths;
convexity is what lets $\tau_P(H)$ be bounded by linear programs, as below.
In the language of linear feasibility, the minimal non-closable sets are
irreducible infeasible subsystems (IIS) and $\tau_P(H)$ is a minimum IIS
cover~\cite{parker1996iis,amaldi2003maxfs}; the theorem is the packing bound
of that covering problem, applied to the shifts of turns. The bounds of
separation are what make the shifts of a dislocation non-zero, and the
statement becomes one about parallel rings.

\begin{corollary}[Chains of dislocations]\label{cor:chain}
Let $\gamma_0, \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_m$ be turns and suppose that for $i = 1,
\dots, m$
\[
  \shift_{\gamma_{i-1}}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_i}(\ell) = \varepsilon_i\,
  F_i(\ell) \quad \text{for all balanced } \ell,
\]
with $\varepsilon_i \in \{+1, -1\}$ and forms $F_i$ that are positive on the
whole points of $P$; for instance $F_i$ the length of a staircase
(Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase}) between a vertex of valence three and one of
valence five lying between $\gamma_{i-1}$ and $\gamma_i$
(Propositions~\ref{prop:dislocation} and~\ref{prop:burgers}(b)), the
staircase containing an arc held at length at least one. Let $q$ be the
number of indices $i < m$ with $\varepsilon_{i+1} \neq \varepsilon_i$. Then
for every whole $\ell \in P$ at most $q + 1$ of the turns $\gamma_i$ are rings:
at least $m - q$ are helices. In particular $m + 1$ nested parallel turns
separated by $m$ dislocations whose staircases all climb the same way leave at
least $m$ helices.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $\gamma_i$ and $\gamma_j$, $i < j$, are both rings, then $\sum_{k=i+1}^{j}
\varepsilon_k F_k(\ell) = \shift_{\gamma_i}(\ell) - \shift_{\gamma_j}(\ell) =
0$; the $F_k(\ell)$ being positive, the signs $\varepsilon_{i+1}, \dots,
\varepsilon_j$ are not all equal, so a change of sign lies between $i$ and
$j$. Between two consecutive rings of the chain lies a change of sign of its
own, so there are at most $q + 1$ rings. (Equivalently, in
Theorem~\ref{thm:forced}, every pair of rings of a monotone stretch is an
infeasible pair.)
\end{proof}

Several chains, or disjoint conflicts of any of the kinds of
Section~\ref{sec:conflicts}, add up by the second part of the theorem. The
corollary is stated with signs $\varepsilon_i$ and forms $F_i$ that the
singular vertices between the turns determine: by
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b), $\shift_{\gamma_{i-1}} - \shift_{\gamma_i}$
is the across component of the Burgers vector of the annulus between them,
computed from the positions and valences of its singular vertices alone.

\begin{remark}[What the bound counts]
The theorem counts turns with a non-zero shift. At the lengths where a turn
is a strip of the mesh (Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}) a non-zero shift is a
helix; at other lengths the same walk may not be a strip. One spiral strip
may also carry several turns of $H$, and the measure of
Section~\ref{sec:measure} counts edge loops that spiral within four quads,
which is yet another count. The bound is therefore a bound on turns, and is
compared below with the turns the engine leaves shifted.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The bound is a transversal number.}
The least number of turns left shifted is a covering number of the minimal
conflicting sets, and it is attained. This is the standard correspondence
between minimum IIS covers and maximum feasible
subsystems~\cite{amaldi2003maxfs,parker1996iis}, stated here for turns.

\begin{theorem}[Forced shifts as a transversal number; standard]\label{thm:transversal}
Call $Y \subseteq H$ \emph{closable} in $P$ if some $\ell \in P$ has
$\shift_\gamma(\ell) = 0$ for all $\gamma \in Y$, and a \emph{core} a set that
is not closable while all its proper subsets are. For any $P$:
(i) $H \setminus X$ is closable if and only if $X$ meets every core; hence
$\tau_P(H)$ is the least size of a set meeting every core;
(ii) for every $\ell \in P$ the turns $\ell$ leaves shifted meet every core;
(iii) every least such set is exactly the set of turns left shifted by some
$\ell \in P$;
(iv) $P \subseteq P'$ implies $\tau_P(H) \ge \tau_{P'}(H)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Closability is hereditary. If $X$ misses a core, $H \setminus X$ contains it;
if $H \setminus X$ is not closable it contains a minimal non-closable subset,
a core, which $X$ misses. The zero-shift set of $\ell$ is closed by $\ell$, so
its complement meets every core. For a least $X$, a length closing
$H \setminus X$ shifts a subset of $X$ meeting every core, hence all of $X$.
Part (iv) is immediate.
\end{proof}

So $\tau_P(H)$ lies between the largest number of disjoint cores, the lower
bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:forced}, and the least hitting set of the cores
found so far, which is exact as soon as its complement is closable: an
\emph{implicit hitting-set} loop alternates the two. It is the algorithm of
Parker and Ryan~\cite{parker1996iis}, an instance of the implicit
hitting-set approach~\cite{morenocenteno2013implicit}; we find cores by the
deletion filter of Chinneck and Dravnieks~\cite{chinneck1991locating}.

\begin{proposition}[Whole lengths and size without a cap]\label{prop:whole-size}
If $P$ is given by the balances and bounds $\ell \ge b \ge 0$, a set closable
in real lengths is closable in whole lengths, and closable in real lengths at
any number of quads greater than $\inf_{P_Y} Q$, and at $\inf_{P_Y} Q$ itself
when the infimum is attained, where $P_Y$ is the set of lengths of $P$ closing
$Y$ and $Q(\ell)$ the number of quads.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Scale a rational point of $P_Y$ by its denominator
(Remark~\ref{rem:exist}). For a number of quads $q$, take $\ell \in P_Y$
with $0 < Q(\ell) \le q$; for $c \ge 1$, $c\ell \in P_Y$ and $Q(c\ell) =
c^2 Q(\ell)$, a continuous function of $c$ that reaches $q$.
\end{proof}

With a cap on the deviation, $P_Y$ is a polytope and $Q(P_Y)$ an interval,
since $P_Y$ is convex and $Q$ continuous: two exact points of $P_Y$, one on
each side of a window of sizes, certify that $Y$ closes within it. Certifying
that it does not needs the extremes of $Q$ over a polytope, an indefinite
bilinear program, for which the McCormick relaxation was of no use here. The
cap acts as a budget shared by all the turns: with $D = 0.474$ the lengths
may move by $\sum_a |\ell_a - t_a| \le 980.7$ edges in all, while closing the
$109$ strips of way~2 that the bounds alone do not force costs $1{,}680.2$
(the balances alone $176.4$). The cores then become covers of a knapsack, of
$1$, $50$ and $49$ turns for the first three disjoint ones, and the savings
of closing several turns are not additive (measured).

\subsection{Choosing the turns}\label{sec:choosing}

The heuristic leaves most of the gap. One may hope for an exact method fast
enough for the engine; in general there is none, unless P${}={}$NP. Call
\RC{} the problem: given the balances $B$, bounds $b$, a set
$H$ of linear forms (the shifts) and a number $k$, are there lengths in $P =
\{\ell : B\ell = 0,\ \ell \ge b\}$ that close at least $k$ forms of $H$?
Choosing the most equations of a linear system that hold together, some
constraints being mandatory, is the maximum feasible subsystem problem,
NP-hard and hard to approximate in general~\cite{amaldi1995complexity};
minimum covers of irreducible infeasible subsystems are strongly NP-hard
already for network flow systems~\cite{joormann2015flowiis}. The theorem
shows that the hardness survives the special structure of quantization:
balances of a bidirected graph, bounds $0$ and $1$, forms with coefficients
$0$ and $1$; the general case is known, this restricted case is new as far
as we found.

\begin{theorem}[Choosing the turns is NP-hard]\label{thm:nphard}
\RC{} is NP-complete, in real or in whole lengths, already when
$B$ is the incidence matrix of a bidirected graph, the bounds are $0$ or $1$,
and the forms have coefficients $0$ or $1$; it is strongly NP-hard, and stays
NP-hard with a cap on the deviation, which may be loose. Moreover
$\tau_P(H)$ can equal the vertex cover number of any graph, with $|H|$ its
number of vertices. So it cannot be approximated within $\sqrt2 - \varepsilon$
unless P${}={}$NP~\cite{khot2017independent,dinur2018towards,dinur2018nonoptimally,khot2018grassmann}, nor within
$2 - \varepsilon$ if the unique games conjecture holds~\cite{khot2008vertex},
and the largest closable subset of $H$ cannot be approximated within
$|H|^{1-\varepsilon}$ unless P${}={}$NP~\cite{hastad1999clique,zuckerman2007linear}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Membership: given a set $Y$ of $k$ forms, closing them in $P$ is the
feasibility of a linear program, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:whole-size} real and whole closability agree. Hardness,
by reduction from \textup{\textsc{Independent Set}}: for a graph $G$, take for each
edge $e = uv$ four arcs $y_{u,e}$, $y_{v,e}$, $d_e$, $d'_e$ with the three
balances
\[
  y_{u,e} + y_{v,e} - d_e = 0, \qquad y_{u,e} + y_{v,e} - d'_e = 0, \qquad
  d_e - d'_e = 0 ,
\]
in which every arc appears in exactly two rows with coefficients $\pm1$, the
bounds $d_e, d'_e \ge 1$ and $y \ge 0$, and for each vertex $u$ the form
$\shift_u = \sum_{e \ni u} y_{u,e}$. With $y \ge 0$, $\shift_u = 0$ forces
$y_{u,e} = 0$ on every edge at $u$. If two adjacent vertices $u$, $v$ are
closed, the edge $uv$ gets $d_e = y_{u,e} + y_{v,e} = 0 < 1$. Conversely, for
an independent set $Y$, every edge has at most one end in $Y$: put $y = 0$ on
the side of that end and $1$ on the other side, and on an edge with no end in
$Y$ put $y_{u,e} = 1$ and $y_{v,e} = 0$ (either way); then $d_e = d'_e = 1$ on
every edge, and these are whole lengths in $P$ closing $Y$.
So the closable sets are exactly the independent sets of $G$, the largest has
the size of the independence number, and $\tau_P(H) = |V| - \alpha(G)$ is the
vertex cover number. The instance has size linear in $G$ and numbers $0$ and
$\pm1$. A cap large enough constrains nothing.
\end{proof}

We checked the reduction by computer on $40$ random graphs, comparing the
closable sets with the independent sets (measured). Its third balance is
redundant over the reals; it is there so that every arc lies in two balances,
as in a T-mesh. The best known hardness of approximating vertex cover without
a conjecture was $1.3606$~\cite{dinur2005hardness}, before $\sqrt2 -
\varepsilon$. The theorem is about the abstract problem: whether the
instances that T-meshes produce are as hard is open.

\begin{conjecture}[Realizable instances]\label{conj:realize}
\RC{} stays NP-hard when $B$, $b$ and $H$ are the balances,
the bounds of separation and the turns of a T-mesh of a closed surface of
genus zero.
\end{conjecture}

The evidence is that vertex cover stays NP-hard on planar graphs of small
degree~\cite{garey1976simplified}, and that two turns separated by a
dislocation conflict like the two ends of an edge
(Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}).

\paragraph{What is easy.}
Several parts of the problem are polynomial. Closing a given set $Y$ is a
linear program. Without turns and without a window, quantization in whole
lengths is an integer minimum-cost flow on a bidirected graph, a matching
problem in the sense of Edmonds and Johnson~\cite{edmonds1970matching}, which
is polynomial; the exact refinement of~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf} works in
this setting. When the cores are known and disjoint, $\tau_P(H)$ is their
number; when every core has two turns and the graph of conflicts is
bipartite, it is a maximum matching (K\H{o}nig). When the shift forms span,
modulo the balances, a space of fixed dimension $d$, the closable sets are
the flats of an arrangement and can be listed in time polynomial for fixed
$d$; but $d$ is at most twice the number of singular vertices, and on the
male body the held turns of a way have rank $52$ of $52$ and the strips $107$
of $111$, so this does not help. The implicit hitting-set loop above is exact
and fast when the cores are small, as with the bounds alone ($66$ seconds at
most per family); with the cap the problem is a budgeted maximum feasible
subsystem with a lattice on top, and the cores grow.

\begin{remark}[The window of sizes; a sketch]
The window on the number of quads makes even closing nothing hard in the weak
sense. Take a disjoint union of tori, each of two patches whose sides have
the targets $N v_i$ and $1$, so that $Q = \sum_i 2 a_i c_i$, and a cap that
lets the short sides $c_i$ grow by $T_0$ edges in all. For $N$ large, the
window $Q = 2N(\sum_i v_i + T_0)$ is met exactly when $\sum_i v_i(c_i - 1) =
T_0$ with whole $c_i \ge 1$: unbounded subset sum, which is
NP-complete~\cite{lueker1975two}, with numbers exponential in the size of the
instance (the gadgets are of genus one and disconnected). Whether the
window alone is strongly NP-hard, with $Q$ polynomial in the size, is open,
as is the complexity of maximizing $Q$ over a polytope of lengths in reals.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{An exact selection, end to end.}
Appendix~\ref{app:endtoend} measures what an exact selection buys on the
bench: a mixed-integer program that closes as many strips as it can, within
the engine's cap and budget, gives lengths that do not spiral less than the
engine's, but good extra candidates for the engine to choose among (from
$4.54\%$ to $2.32\%$ and $2.70\%$ of spiral edges over $36$ runs), and only
when the solver finishes: with a limit of $5$ seconds per solve the gain
vanishes.

\section{What is machine-checked}\label{sec:lean}

The folder \texttt{docs/research/closing-helices/lean} of the repository is
a Lean~4~\cite{demoura2021lean4} project on Mathlib~\cite{mathlib2020},
pinned to Mathlib \texttt{v4.35.0} and the toolchain
\texttt{leanprover/lean4:v4.35.0}; \texttt{make proofs} fetches Mathlib
compiled and checks it. It holds $187$ theorem declarations, many of them
small lemmas, a few of them tautologies that only restate a hypothesis (such
as \texttt{dislocation\_closes\_iff} or \texttt{staircase\_rings}); the count is
not evidence, and the folder's \texttt{README.md} maps each statement of the
note to its main Lean theorems. Every theorem depends only on Lean's
standard axioms. What Lean checks is the algebraic and combinatorial core of
each statement below, with the topological facts it uses (that a loop
develops into a given motion, that a curve bounds a disc, that the
fundamental group of $\Sigma^\circ$ is presented by the loops $\lambda_i$,
that the quad graph of a sphere is bipartite) as explicit hypotheses of the
theorem that uses them.

\begin{itemize}
\item Checked as algebra: the Burgers sum and the order of first visit
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}), dislocations, staircases, exchanges and
  the conjugation step of Proposition~\ref{prop:winding}; the checkerboard
  rules, the induction of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:parity} given a
  2-colouring, and the second proof of Remark~\ref{rem:borders}; the
  Fox--Burgers formula, its link with Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a) for
  a simple turn, its corollary and the commutator; the handshake on a
  closed surface, Proposition~\ref{prop:free} (stronger than stated), the
  Farkas certificates of Remarks~\ref{rem:definite} and~\ref{rem:exist},
  Theorem~\ref{thm:forced} and Corollary~\ref{cor:chain}; for fields of any
  order, the rotation part of Gauss--Bonnet, the Gauss--Bonnet counts of
  triangle and hexagonal meshes, the lengths of dislocations and the
  congruences of Section~\ref{sec:beyond}.
\item Checked in a weaker form than the statement:
  Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}, only the additivity of the composed
  translation given additive crossings (the integer coefficients and the
  reading as a helix are not formalized); Lemmas~\ref{lem:gauss}
  and~\ref{lem:eight}, only the Euler-characteristic count they use and, for
  Lemma~\ref{lem:eight}, the totals $3, 3, 2$ and $2, 2, 4$ it gives from the
  numbers of convex and concave corners of each region (that the regions are
  discs, and which corners are convex, the case analysis of the crossing, are
  hypotheses); Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}, only in a
  model where the strips are \emph{defined} as the cycles of a successor map

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{problem}[Exact formulation]
Minimize the deviation of the quantization subject to balance, validity and
$\shift_\gamma(\ell)=0$ for a set of turns. This is a minimum-deviation flow
with linear side constraints; how much of the exact machinery
of~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf} carries over is open. Section~\ref{sec:exact}
shows that the linear relaxation already closes almost all held turns; with
the number of turns closed as the objective, the problem is NP-hard
(Theorem~\ref{thm:nphard}).
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Closing in whole numbers]
The solutions of the balance and shift equations in whole numbers form a
lattice, and Appendix~\ref{sec:whole} searched it through a reduced basis
and the reformulation of~\cite{aardal2000diophantine}: whole lengths closing
every ring asked for are found, but in minutes, with the number of quads
held only to first order, and at a deviation $0.14$ to $0.22$ above the real
optimum. Can the integer search of the quantizer move within this lattice at
the speed of a flow solver, with the number of quads held exactly? This
matters once the right turns are chosen; Appendix~\ref{sec:whole} suggests it
is not, on its own, where the loops are lost.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Which turns]
This is, on the evidence of Appendix~\ref{sec:whole}, the central problem:
closing rings changes the lengths, the lengths change the strips, and the
strips of the new mesh are other walks. The straight walks that return are
infinite in number; we sample them, and Appendix~\ref{sec:whole} shows the
sample misses most of the loops that spiral. Choosing the most turns to
close is NP-hard (Theorem~\ref{thm:nphard}), and closing the most rings, even
exactly and keeping each ring the same walk, gives good candidates only when
the solver finishes, and does not by itself make fewer spirals
(Section~\ref{sec:choosing}, Appendix~\ref{app:endtoend}). Is there a finite set of turns, a basis in
some homological sense of the regular region of the T-mesh, whose closing
implies that every loop of the quad mesh closes? Can the loops of the quad
mesh be read off the T-mesh and its lengths directly, so that every one is
accounted for? Which objective, short of all loops, predicts the spirals
the measure sees?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Feasibility]
On a human torso, some held turns cannot be closed together: the projection
lets them go. Our hypothesis was that pairs of singularities between two
rings force a dislocation, and that closing all rings needs the poles to
face one another, which the separation of singularities forbids at the
scale of the T-mesh; Propositions~\ref{prop:dislocation}
and~\ref{prop:staircase} prove this for a single pair joined by a straight
chain or a staircase. A capped cylinder shows the same conflict: its
layout of six patches exists, but only with arcs our quantizer will not
bring to zero. On the male body the conflict comes down to one arc in four
of the eight ways, to 9 to 30 arcs in the others (Section~\ref{sec:exact}),
and to irreducible sets of one to fifteen turns; the smallest are
dislocations, exchanges of two vertices, caps and a winding separatrix
(Section~\ref{sec:conflicts}). When is a set of turns simultaneously
closable, in terms of the singularity configuration?
Every certificate is a sum of combs (Proposition~\ref{thm:combs}), which says
nothing about the singularities; restricted to staircases, caps and winding
separatrices it is not (Section~\ref{sec:combs}). A useful answer must lie
between: for instance a decomposition into combs on disjoint sets of
vertices, each of the sign of the certificate.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Lower bounds with the size]
Theorem~\ref{thm:forced} bounds the shifted turns any quantization leaves
within a set of lengths, and on the male body the engine's bounds of
separation force exactly one to nine held turns. With the cap and the window of sizes the values are known
only within wide intervals (Table~\ref{tab:forced}): a closable set at a size
is certified by two exact points, but its absence needs the extremes of the
number of quads, an indefinite quadratic function, over a polytope. How can
$\tau_P(H)$ be computed for quantizations of a given size and deviation?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Complexity]
Theorem~\ref{thm:nphard} is about abstract instances. Is
\RC{} still NP-hard on the instances of T-meshes of a closed
sphere (Conjecture~\ref{conj:realize}), or for a fixed number of singular
vertices? Is the window of sizes alone strongly NP-hard, with a number of
quads polynomial in the size of the instance, and what is the complexity of
the largest number of quads over a polytope of lengths? And when the set of
turns depends on the lengths, as the strips of the mesh do, what is the
problem at all?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Finite trajectories]
In the language of~\cite{lei2020quartic}: given a singularity configuration
on a closed surface of genus zero, when does a quartic differential with all
trajectories finite exist, and how close to a given cross field can it be
chosen? Our turns are its discrete, integer version.
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Towards proofs.}
The algebraic and combinatorial cores of Sections~\ref{sec:turns}
and~\ref{sec:obstructions} to~\ref{sec:forced} are machine-checked
(Section~\ref{sec:lean}); their topological hypotheses are not, nor the
results on combs, borders and complexity. Proposition~\ref{prop:linear} is elementary but its statement over a
T-mesh with T-junctions is easy to get wrong. A formal model of an abstract
complex of rectangles with integer side lengths, its turns and its developing
map would turn those hypotheses into theorems, and could carry the
sufficiency question of the third problem for special classes of layouts.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
The method is implemented in openclare (Rust), in the main branch of the
repository at the cited commit; the bench is driven by \texttt{bench/run.py}
and the marks by \texttt{scripts/scores.py}. The export of the T-mesh used
in Sections~\ref{sec:exact} to~\ref{sec:forced} and the appendices is an experimental hook of
the branch \texttt{rd-exact}, and the end-to-end selection of
Appendix~\ref{app:endtoend} is on the experimental branch
\texttt{maxclose-rd}; neither is part of the engine, and neither is tagged
yet. The scripts and logs of the exact experiments are not in the
repository (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}): until they are published, the

but not rerun as they stand. The Lean proofs are in
\texttt{docs/research/closing-helices/lean} and replay with
\texttt{make proofs}. This version of the note was built from commit
\texttt{\enginecommit}.

\section{Measured on the male body}\label{sec:measured}

The statements of Section~\ref{sec:obstructions} concern the T-mesh. We
exported it with every turn, for
the eight ways of the male body at 10{,}590 quads: its $1{,}295$ nodes
($1{,}034$ T-junctions and $261$ singular vertices, $133$ of valence three,
$127$ of valence five and one of valence two, of total index $8$) with their
corner angles, its $2{,}069$ arcs with their traces, its $776$ patches, and
for each turn the arcs it crosses. A turn is drawn as a curve by straight
chords across the patches it crosses, and the regions its curve cuts the
surface into are found from the chords. Identities between quantities of
this appendix are identities between linear forms in the arc lengths modulo
the balances, checked on a basis of the balanced lengths in floating point;
parities are evaluated at the whole lengths the engine found. Counts are
summed over the eight ways, a turn found in several ways counting once in
each (measured throughout).

\paragraph{What holds.}
For each of the $1{,}299$ turns, the crossing maps composed along the walk
give a translation whose component across is the exported shift
(Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}). Of these turns, $718$ have a simple curve;
each splits the $261$ singular vertices into two groups of total index $4$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss}). On each side of each of them, with a tree of cuts
found by breadth-first search, the holonomy of the curve equals the
composition of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a), in the order of first visit,
in both components: $1{,}436$ discs holding $4$ to $257$ singular vertices,
no exception. The order matters: taking the vertices in the order of their
last visit fails on $150$ of the $158$ discs of way~2. The checkerboard rule
(Proposition~\ref{prop:checkerboard}) holds on the $1{,}436$ discs, in the
form given after Corollary~\ref{cor:parity}, and on the $30{,}148$ annuli
between two disjoint simple turns, whose singular vertices have total index
$0$ in every case, as Lemma~\ref{lem:gauss} says. The $294$ pairs of
disjoint turns with no singular vertex between them have equal shifts
(Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(c)). Ten annuli hold exactly one vertex of
valence three and one of valence five; on all ten the identity of
Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(i) holds, and in none of them do the two
vertices lie on one trace, so part (ii) never applies at the real lengths.
The parity of Proposition~\ref{prop:parity} holds for the $1{,}299$ turns.

\paragraph{Most held turns are not simple.}
Of the $1{,}299$ turns, $101$ cross themselves once, as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:eight}: $97$ split the singular vertices as $(3, 3, 2)$, and
$4$ as $(2, 2, 4)$, all four crossing their closing segment. The other $480$
cross themselves at least twice, up to $107$ times. Of the $765$ turns held
for closing, only $356$ are simple: long turns cross themselves many times,
and the statements about simple curves and annuli say nothing about them.
For such curves we use winding numbers: a combination $\sum_\gamma u_\gamma
\bar\gamma$ of curves winds round each region of their union a whole number
of times. This is its class in the homology of $\Sigma^\circ$; it tells round
which singular vertices the combination goes, but not its holonomy, which
is not commutative.

\section{Moving singularities in the parametrization}\label{sec:move}

Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers} suggests a lever before quantization: move
the singular vertices so that the Burgers sum of each ring vanishes. We tested
it on the seamless maps of the development models (measured throughout this
appendix; the experiment is a patch of the engine, not part of it).

\paragraph{The Burgers sum on other models.} For every ring of the seamless
map that leans by at most $0.12$, we computed the sum of
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a) over the singular vertices of its smaller
side, cut open along a tree of faces and ordered by first visit along its
border. On seven closed surfaces of genus zero (blob, rock, creature, spot,
pig, ogre, blub), $3{,}600$ of the $3{,}604$ rings that bound a disc are
reproduced to $10^{-5}(1+L)$, $L$ the length of the ring; the reading fails
only where a side is not a disc (borders on suzanne, handles on bob, part and
cage). With the border and period terms of
Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers-any} it holds there too
(Section~\ref{sec:beyond}).

\paragraph{Moving a singularity does not move the shift.} The linearization
$\delta \shift = \langle e_2, Q_i(I-R_i)\,\delta p_i\rangle$ assumes the other
singular vertices keep their developed positions. They do not: on the blob
at $3{,}000$ quads, moving one pole of a cap one edge changes the shift of
the cap ring (length $30.7$, shift $-1.065$) by $-0.13$ with a local re-solve
of the map, where the sum predicts $-0.60$; with a global re-solve it moved
by $+0.14$, the other way, against a prediction of $-0.58$: the other poles
of the cap move in the map and absorb the change. Over twelve moves per
model, the measured change is $0.12$, $0.03$ and $0.01$ of the predicted one
on the blob, the creature and the pig. Rotating the field rigidly by $\alpha
= |\shift|/L$ on the smaller side instead moves the shift by $26\%$, $36\%$,
$46\%$, $64\%$ and $87\%$ of $\alpha L$ as the rotated region grows by $0$,
$5$, $10$, $20$ and $40$ rings of vertices, at almost no cost in distortion
($0.3362$ to $0.3371$). By Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(c) the shift is a property of
the whole cylinder: shearing a long cylinder is expensive in the
least-squares map, displacing a cone point in it is not, so the map keeps the
shift the field gives the cylinder and moves its cone points instead. The
identity holds exactly; it is the developed positions of the cone points that
the field sets, not their places on the surface.

\paragraph{A lever that fails.} Cancelling the pairs of valences three and
five of a twisted annulus would change the shift by its $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}); its effect on the spirals of the
meshes was not recorded, and we do not report it. Rotating the field
cylinder by cylinder to bring the shifts to zero reduced the shifts of the
map but moved the twist onto rings of $90$ to $200$ edges, whose sensitivity
grows with their length: over $36$ runs ($12$ models, $3$ sizes), $2$
improved and $4$ regressed, the mean gain coming from a single run, which we
read as chaos rather than progress. Our reading, a conjecture: moving
singularities would act through the Burgers sum only in a map whose scale is
free to follow them, a cone metric; with a map of fixed scale the lever is
the orientation of the field along whole cylinders, long rings must be
handled jointly, and the conflicts are better attacked in quantization,
where the staircases and dislocations of Section~\ref{sec:obstructions} live.


\section{Rotations of other orders}\label{app:orders}

\paragraph{Rotations of order six.}
On triangle and hexagonal meshes the rotations are powers of $\zeta = e^{i\pi/3}$
and whole lengths develop nodes onto the Eisenstein integers; a vertex of
valence $k$ has index $6 - k$, in sixths of a turn, and the indices add up to
$6\chi$. A turn walks along
one of six directions $u$ and comes back translated by $T = \alpha u + \shift\,
\zeta u$: $\shift$ counts the rows of the family of lines along $u$, $\alpha$
depends on the oblique basis chosen, and in the basis $(u, \zeta u)$ the
strip has $2\alpha + \shift$ triangles per turn. The Burgers sum, the order of first visit and the
Fox--Burgers formula hold unchanged. A pair of valences five and seven has
$B = \pm\zeta^{\pm1} d$, of length $|d|$, and the difference of the shifts is
$\pm d_1$ or $\pm(d_1 + d_2)$ with $d = d_1 u + d_2 \zeta u$: the two turns close
together only if the pair lies along one of the two other line directions, and
a pair joined along a line parallel to the turns always forces a helix.
Staircases have three directions, two that climb and one along which the form
of the shift difference vanishes. There is no parity: $1 - \zeta$ is a unit.
When every singular vertex has even valence the rotations have order three and
the congruence modulo $(1 - \omega)^2 = -3\omega$ (compare Lemma~11
of~\cite{izmestiev2013torus}, on holonomy of order three) gives, on a closed
sphere,
$\alpha \equiv \shift \pmod 3$; every ring has a length divisible by three, as on
subdivided octahedra (rings of $3n$ edges), while on subdivided icosahedra rings
of $5n$ edges take every residue (measured on subdivided octahedra and
icosahedra). Whether a rule modulo three holds
with borders or handles is a conjecture we leave open.

\paragraph{Other rotation orders.}
Nothing in the Burgers sum, the order of first visit or the Fox--Burgers
formula uses that $R$ has order four: they hold for the rotations of order
six of triangle and hexagonal meshes, for cone metrics of any angles, and for
motions of space. What changes is parity. When every rotation is a power
$z^{j_i}$ of one rotation $z$, every Fox derivative is congruent to its
exponent sum modulo $1 - z$, and $1 - z^j$ to $j(1 - z)$ modulo $(1 - z)^2$, so
\[
  \tau(w) \equiv (1 - z) \sum_i \epsilon\Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial
  x_i}\Bigr) j_i\, p_i \pmod{(1 - z)^2},
\]
proved in any commutative ring. For quarter-turns, $(1 - z)^2 = -2z$, and this
is Corollary~\ref{cor:checker}, with $j_i = 4 - k(c_i)$. For rotations of order
three, $(1 - z)^2 = -3z$: a rule modulo three, with three colours. For
rotations of order six, $1 - z$ is a unit: there is no colouring rule on
triangle or hexagonal meshes. A dislocation of order $n$ has $|B| = 2\sin(\pi
/ n)\,|d|$: $\sqrt2\,|d|$ for quads, $|d|$ for a pair of valences five and
seven on a triangle mesh, $\sqrt3\,|d|$ for rotations of order three; the
forms $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$ are particular to quads. Discrete Gauss--Bonnet reads
$\sum (4 - k) = 4\chi$ on quad meshes, $\sum (6 - k) = 6\chi$ on triangle
meshes and $\sum (3 - k) = 3\chi$ on hexagonal ones.
Theorem~\ref{thm:forced}, Corollary~\ref{cor:chain} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:handshake} do not depend on the geometry at all.


\section{Whole lengths}\label{sec:whole}

This appendix returns to the male body of Section~\ref{sec:exact}, now in
whole numbers. The propositions are proved; the numbers are measured, with
exact integer or rational arithmetic where we say so, and otherwise from
HiGHS through SciPy, stopped at a time limit (best solution found and proven
bound).

\paragraph{Exact whole lengths.}
Closing exactly, in whole numbers, the turns found closable together with
the bounds kept is feasible: HiGHS solves it in about two minutes a way for
three of the four ways tried (the fourth, with 87 turns, did not finish
within ten minutes). The solutions leave 1 to 4 held turns shifted where the engine
leaves 13 to 44, and fewer of the turns not held as well (5 against 9, 5
against 11, 0 against 27), at a deviation from the real lengths 4\% to 62\%
higher. Yet the quad meshes they make spiral \emph{more}: 12.2\% of their
edges against 8.8\% for the first way, 3.7\% against 1.1\% for the second,
and the third breaks the structure. Most of the loops that spiral are thus
not turns our search finds: closing every turn it finds is not enough.

\paragraph{All the strips of a mesh.}
Walking every strip of the mesh the engine of the export makes for way~2
(Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}) finds 230 ways round; each strip is found
twice, once in each direction, with opposite shift expressions (the surface
being oriented), and 168 of them come back shifted. A selection of rings by
the field's shift alone (under half a row) is too narrow: the twisted thigh,
shifted 1.65 rows a turn by the field, is a ring a modeller wants closed.
Selecting rings by their lean, as the constraint rounds of the engine now do
(Section~\ref{sec:closing}), gives 222 of the 230 ways round; 218 of them
(109 strips) can be closed together in real numbers with every bound of
separation kept. Posed directly, HiGHS found no whole solution within ten
minutes, nor in a later run of thirty. The rest of this appendix explains why, and what that set of rings
really costs.

\paragraph{The balances form a bidirected graph.}
The matrix $B$ has two rows per patch, one per direction, 1{,}552 rows for
the 776 patches, and one column per arc. On a closed surface where no patch
borders itself across an arc, an arc lies on a side of each of the two
patches it borders, so its column has exactly two non-zero entries,
each $+1$ or $-1$ according to the side of the patch it lies on (measured on
all eight systems; in way 2, 958 arcs have signs $+/-$, 557 have $-/-$ and
554 have $+/+$). In the language of signed graphs, $B$ is the incidence
matrix of a \emph{bidirected graph} $G$ whose nodes are the balance equations
and whose edges are the arcs; an arc on a border of the surface has a single
entry, a half-edge of $G$. Bidirected graphs are the setting
of~\cite{heistermann2023bimdf}; the rank of their incidence matrices and the
circuits of the associated matroid are due to
Zaslavsky~\cite{zaslavsky1982signed}, and integer flows are generated by
circuits~\cite{chen2009flow}. Proposition~\ref{prop:free} below makes a
lattice basis explicit. Call a cycle of $G$ \emph{balanced} if values
$\pm1$ on its edges have a zero balance at each of its nodes, and
\emph{unbalanced} otherwise.

\begin{lemma}[Handshake]\label{lem:handshake}
Let $x \in \Z^n$ and $C$ be a connected component of $G$. Then
$\sum_{r \in C} (Bx)_r \equiv \sum_{a} x_a \pmod 2$, the second sum running
over the border arcs of $C$. On a closed surface, $\sum_r (Bx)_r$ is even.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It is $\sum_a x_a \sum_{r \in C} B_{ra}$, and each column sums to $0$ or
$\pm2$, or to $\pm1$ for a border arc.
\end{proof}

So on a closed surface the rows of $B$ are dependent modulo $2$, although they
are independent over the reals: a system $Bx = d$ with $\sum_r d_r$ odd has
real solutions but no whole one; and, $G$ being connected with an unbalanced
cycle, one with an even sum always has whole solutions
(on the edges $T$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:free}, the whole image of $B$ is
exactly the lattice of vectors of even sum; Section~\ref{sec:lean}). Consistently, integer elimination of $B$ ends on the male
body with one row whose entries have greatest common divisor $2$, all other
pivots being units (measured in all eight ways). In general the number of such
rows is the number of components of $G$ with an unbalanced cycle and no
half-edge: none on Suzanne and Jerry, whose borders give half-edges, one on
Bob, part, cage and a cylinder (measured). This does not affect the balanced
lengths:

\begin{proposition}[Free arcs]\label{prop:free}
Let $G$ be connected and contain an unbalanced cycle. Let $T$ be the edges
of a spanning tree of $G$ and one more edge closing an unbalanced cycle, and
$F$ the other edges. Then $B$ has full row rank, and every $x_F \in \Z^F$
extends to exactly one $x \in \Z^n$ with $Bx = 0$. The whole balanced lengths
thus form a lattice isomorphic to $\Z^F$, of dimension $n - m$ for $m$
balances, with a basis of vectors $z_f$, $f \in F$, each supported on
$T \cup \{f\}$ with $z_f(f) = 1$ and entries in $\{0, \pm1, \pm2\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Such a $T$ exists: the sign of a cycle is a homomorphism from the cycle space
of $G$ over $\Z/2$, of which the fundamental cycles of a spanning tree are a
basis, so if all of them were balanced every cycle would be. $T$ has $m$
edges. If $B_T y = 0$, a node met by a single edge of $T$ forces
that edge to zero; removing such edges repeatedly leaves the unbalanced
cycle, on which each value fixes the next up to sign, and going round, since
the cycle is unbalanced, forces them all to zero. So the square matrix $B_T$
is invertible, $B$ has full row rank, and $x_F$ has exactly one real
extension; it remains to see that it is whole. For $f \in F$, the graph $T
\cup \{f\}$ has a one-dimensional space of balanced values. Removing the edges
met at a node of degree one leaves either three paths between two nodes,
whose three cycles are the unbalanced cycle of $T$, one more unbalanced and
one balanced (the number of unbalanced cycles of such a graph is even), or
two cycles joined by a path, possibly of length zero. In the first case the
balanced cycle contains $f$, since the cycle of $T$ does not, and carries
values $\pm1$. In the second, the cycle through $f$ either is balanced and
carries values $\pm1$, or is unbalanced, and then values $\pm1$ on both cycles
and $\pm2$ on the joining path are balanced. Either way there is a whole
balanced $z_f$ supported on $T \cup \{f\}$ with $z_f(f) = \pm1$; changing its
sign if needed, $x = \sum_{f \in F} x_f z_f$ is the whole extension.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis can fail beyond the closed sphere: on a torus without
singular vertex and on part, $B$ does not have full row rank ($4$ of $6$ and
$145$ of $148$, measured). The proposition extends with a spanning forest of
$G$ and, in each component with an unbalanced cycle, one edge closing such a
cycle or one half-edge; the whole balanced lengths then form a lattice of
dimension $n - m$ plus the number of balanced components without half-edge.

On the male body $G$ is connected with an unbalanced cycle, $B$ has rank
1{,}552, and the lattice of balanced lengths has dimension $2{,}069 - 1{,}552
= 517$ (measured in all eight ways). Balances alone thus never obstruct whole
lengths: 517 arcs can be given any whole lengths and the others follow. With
the shifts of the 218 rings of way 2 the rank grows by 105 and the lattice of
closed quantizations has dimension 412; integer elimination still uses unit
pivots only, with one row of greatest common divisor $2$ (the shift of one
ring is even as soon as the balances and the rings before it hold). Adding
the shifts of the held turns instead gives dimensions 469, 439, 465, 431, 435,
363 and 431 in ways 0 to 6; in ways 4, 5 and 6 elimination needs steps of
Euclid's algorithm, so turn rows can destroy the free-arc structure.

\paragraph{Integer solutions exist.}
By Remark~\ref{rem:exist}, whole lengths closing the 218
rings within the bounds exist. HiGHS finds some in one second once it is
told which arcs are zero in \emph{every} real solution: closing the 218
rings with $\ell \ge 0$ forces 171 arcs to zero (measured), the implicit
equalities of the cone, each certified by a non-negative combination of
shifts as in Remark~\ref{rem:definite}. There are 23 distinct certificates:
13 single rings whose shift is sign-definite (round fingers and toes), 4
pairs with multipliers $(1, 1)$ (left foot, right foot, head, top of the
head), and 6 combinations of four to six rings (torso, neck). Given as upper
bounds, these zeros make the problem easy; left implicit, the solver works on
a face of lower dimension than the problem without knowing it, which is what
the ten- and thirty-minute failures were. They are not an integer
obstruction.

\paragraph{The difficulty is their size.}
The 171 arcs forced to zero carry 350.8 quad edges of target length, the
longest 10.1. The real lengths closing the 218 rings nearest the targets, in
the weighted deviation, make 3{,}960 quads instead of the 10{,}496 of the
targets. With the deviation the engine measures, the mean of
$|\ell_a - \lambda r_a|$, a linear program shows that no real lengths closing
the 218 rings have a mean deviation below $0.817$, nor, after dropping 7
strips (14 ways round) so that every arc with a target of at least $1.5$
stays open, below $0.758$; balanced lengths without rings reach $0.085$. A
lower bound in real numbers holds for whole numbers too. The engine version
that made the export refused quantizations whose deviation exceeded $1.5$
times that of the plain search, $1.5 \times 0.316 = 0.474$; the engine at
the cited commit allows $\max(1.5 \times 0.316,\ 0.316 + 0.2) = 0.516$. Either
way the 218 rings cannot close at the size asked for within the engine's
tolerance, whatever the rounding: the obstruction is in the reals.

\paragraph{The 190 rings that fit.}
A subset of 190 of the 218 ways round (chosen greedily, so 190 is only a
lower bound on what fits) closes in real numbers at a mean
deviation of $0.399$, keeping the number of quads (10{,}502) at that of the
targets to first order: the linear form $\nabla q(t) \cdot \ell \ge \nabla
q(t) \cdot t$, where $q(\ell)$ is the number of quads, a quadratic function of
the lengths, and $t$ the targets. In whole numbers we followed the
reformulation of Aardal, Hurkens and Lenstra~\cite{aardal2000diophantine}:
a basis $M$ of the lattice of closed quantizations, with the 74 implicit
zeros of this set imposed first (dimension 407), reduced by
LLL~\cite{lenstra1982lll} for a weighted inner product, which leaves entries
in $[-2, 2]$; then $\ell = My$ with $y$ whole, so that no equation is left,
and a mixed-integer program over $y$. Babai's nearest plane~\cite{babai1986nearest}
from the real solution gives a mean deviation of $0.586$ but 10 arcs below
their bounds. HiGHS, stopped after 1{,}200 seconds, gives whole lengths
closing all 190 rings within every bound, with a mean deviation of $0.619$
and 10{,}568 quads, and proves that no whole lengths do better than $0.536$
under the same constraints, the first-order proxy of the number of quads
included. Against $0.399$ in real numbers, this rounding gap is the one
integer obstruction we found, and it is not a parity condition: every
infeasible set of Table~\ref{tab:iis} is infeasible in real numbers already.

\paragraph{Which turns, not rounding.}
Given to the engine in place of the lengths of way 2, these whole lengths
close all 190 rings, with 10{,}568 quads and 2.5\% of spiral edges against
1.1\% for the engine's own lengths (the share of that way alone: the
deviation cap would refuse it). At the new lengths the strips are
different walks: walking all of them finds 230 ways round, 156 of them
shifted (168 at the engine's lengths), and of the 214 that lean less than
$0.12$, 142 are shifted (162 of 222 at the engine's lengths). Closing the
rings of one set of lengths moves the strips, and other loops spiral. On this
body, then, whole lengths are not what is missing: rounding costs between $0.137$
(proven) and $0.220$ (found) of mean deviation above the real optimum, and
it is the choice of the turns to
close, at the lengths where they are the strips of the mesh, that decides how
much the mesh spirals.


\section{Closing the most rings, end to end}\label{app:endtoend}

\paragraph{Most rings closable, end to end.}
Hardness is a statement about the worst case; we also measured what an exact
selection buys on the bench, with the engine of branch
\texttt{complete-helices} (the constraint rounds without the finer work mesh of the
cited commit: $4.54\%$ of spiral edges over the $36$ runs, against $4.65\%$).
We posed, for the lengths the engine keeps, the
mixed-integer program that closes as many of its strips leaning less than
$0.12$ as it can (weighted by their length round), within the balances, the
bounds of separation, the engine's cap on the mean deviation and its quad
budget, and meshed the result with the engine itself; then walked the strips
at the new lengths and solved again, three rounds. Posed with the shift
expressions alone, the program closes $27.3$ of the $38.2$ such strips of a
model (12 models, three budgets each), but at the new lengths only $22.1$ are
closed strips, against $21.8$ for the engine: the strips have become other
walks. Each walk stays the same where the place it crosses every arc stays
inside that arc, half a quad edge from either end, two linear inequalities per
crossing; required for the rings counted as closed, they make the count exact
($24.6$ closed by the program, $24.0$ found closed by walking the mesh) and
show that, within the engine's cap and budget, it closes only about three
strips fewer than can be closed together without changing walk (measured).

\paragraph{What it buys.}
In place of the engine's lengths, these lengths do not spiral less: $4.77\%$
and $5.08\%$ of spiral edges in two runs against $4.54\%$, better on 13 and 10
runs of 36, worse on 10 and 13. Added to the lengths the engine chooses among,
by its own count of spirals (which equals the measured share on every run
checked), they lower the mean to $2.32\%$ and $2.70\%$, never higher. Three
other draws of the engine's per-arc preferences, added the same way, give
$4.12\%$, and three lengths drawn at random within the same cap and budget
$4.34\%$; without the conditions on the walks, the rounds give $3.15\%$, no
better than the other draws (better on 7 runs, worse on 7). The other measures
barely move (closed loops $38.0\%$ against $36.3\%$ of edges, mean distance
$0.383$ against $0.385$, all $36$ meshes valid). On the male body every round
spiralled more than the engine (from $3.45\%$ to $5.59\%$ against $1.59\%$);
there, within the time allowed, the program closed only 35 to 38 of 107
strips (the engine 33), and in 600 seconds at most 48 that keep their walk, 65
that do not, its bound remaining far above (measured). Closing the most rings
is thus not, on its own, the way to fewer helices: the shifted strips the
measure does not see as spirals are among those closed, and others take their
place; it is a source of good candidates, valuable only where the strips
closed stay the strips of the mesh.

\paragraph{Only when the solver finishes.}
The engine can afford a few seconds per solve. With a limit of $5$ seconds the
gain vanishes (an outcome that depends on the hardware and its load,
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}; the distribution of solve times was not
recorded): the engine with three rounds as candidates gives $4.10\%$,
against $4.12\%$ for three other draws (run by run, 2 better and 10 worse than
those draws); $63$ of the $126$ pieces get no solution in $5$ seconds, $21$
more stop at the limit, and $68$ of $108$ rounds return the engine's own
lengths (measured). A local search inside the engine, walks of its strip
network biased by Lagrange multipliers with the conditions on the walks kept,
costs $0.1$ to $0.25$ seconds per model but closes no more rings than the
engine (6 of 30 on the blob, 12 of 26 on the creature, unchanged): a single
walk almost always changes the shift of a ring already closed, and closing
needs combinations of walks. The gain comes from a solver that finishes, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:nphard} says that no exact method finishes fast on every
instance.


\section{What Lean checks, statement by statement}\label{app:lean}

\begin{itemize}
\item For motions whose linear parts lie in any ring acting on any module: the
  translation of $\rho_m \circ \dots \circ \rho_1$ is $\sum_i Q_i(I - R_i)p_i$
  with $Q_i = R_m \cdots R_{i+1}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a)); the
  recursion of the gluing maps of a tree of cuts unfolds into the order of
  first visit, and the order of last visit gives another vector already for
  two vertices; the holonomy of the border of a cut annulus
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(b)); exchanges
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:swap}); the conjugation of
  Proposition~\ref{prop:winding}; the additivity behind
  Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}.
\item Over the reals: the four orders and orientations of a dislocation, the
  forms $\pm d_1 \pm d_2$ and $|B_A|^2 = 2|d|^2$, straight chains
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:dislocation}(i), (ii)); staircases
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:staircase}); the positivity of a non-negative form
  with a held arc (Remark~\ref{rem:definite}).
\item At whole lengths: the parities of $(I - R^{\pm1})p$ and $(I - R^2)p$; the
  checkerboard rule (Proposition~\ref{prop:checkerboard}); the induction of the
  proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:parity}, given a 2-colouring of the quad graph
  (the extension from positive to all whole lengths is on paper),
  and the second proof of Remark~\ref{rem:borders}.
\item The Fox--Burgers formula on words, which is well defined on the free
  group, the fundamental formula, the twisted windings and $\sum_i (I -
  R_i)^\top W_i = 0$, the insertion of the relation, which changes the
  $W_i$ but not the sum (Proposition~\ref{prop:fox}); on the word $x_m
  \cdots x_1$ of distinct letters, $\rho(\partial w/\partial x_i) = Q_i$
  and the formula is the Burgers sum of Proposition~\ref{prop:burgers}(a);
  the commutator
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:commutator}); Corollary~\ref{cor:checker}, with the
  two arguments of its proof on the colour of the origin and the constant of
  the winding numbers.
\item Lemma~\ref{lem:handshake} on a closed surface, Theorem~\ref{thm:forced}
  and Corollary~\ref{cor:chain}.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:free}, with switchings and spanning trees grown by
  leaves, and stronger: on its edges $T$, the whole image of $B$ is the
  lattice of vectors of even sum.
\item Remarks~\ref{rem:definite} and~\ref{rem:exist}: Farkas' lemma by
  Fourier--Motzkin elimination over any ordered field, the arcs forced to zero,
  whole multipliers and whole solutions.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}, in a model only: there the strips are
  defined as the cycles of a successor map built from a permutation of the
  half-integer stations, so the bijection holds by construction; separately,
  the real walk from $k + \frac12$ follows the unit edges in a
  one-dimensional model of the gluing. That the station walk of the gluing
  data is a permutation and that the model is the quad mesh are assumptions.
\item The Euler-characteristic count used in Lemmas~\ref{lem:gauss}
  and~\ref{lem:eight}, on the grid refined by two, and the totals $3, 3, 2$
  and $2, 2, 4$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:eight}, given how many convex and concave
  corners each of its three regions has.
\end{itemize}
Not checked: the topology the discrete statements take as hypotheses (that a
simple closed curve bounds a disc, the developing map and the presentation of
$\pi_1(\Sigma^\circ)$), which corners of the regions of Lemma~\ref{lem:eight}
are convex, strips on surfaces with borders, and every measured statement.


\paragraph{Invariants for the engine.}
The checked identities give tests on the engine's own quantities; since
commit \texttt{3bcbd20} its debug builds check the first three below, and it
uses the parity and the sign-definite turns to spare work
(Section~\ref{sec:closing}):
on a closed surface of genus zero, the two components of the translation of
every turn, its length and its shift, have the same parity at the whole lengths
of the integer search, so every ring has an even number of quads round it; the
coefficients of every shift expression are whole; every column of the balances
has two entries $\pm1$, or one for an arc on a border, so on a closed surface a
balance system with an odd total right-hand side has no whole solution, and in
general the sum over a component has the parity of the sum of the values on
its border arcs; where the developed cone points are known, the
translation of a turn bounding a disc is the Burgers sum in the order of first
visit, and its two components have the parity of the number of vertices of odd
valence on points of odd colour; a turn whose shift is a non-negative form with
a held arc, or both turns of a staircase with a held arc, are never closed; and
the number of held turns left shifted is at least the number of disjoint
infeasible sets found. The README of the folder lists them with the engine's
names.
